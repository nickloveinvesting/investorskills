## A Comprehensive Guide to Acquiring 5-50 Unit Multifamily Properties Without Institutional Leverage

**Context:** 2026 Market Environment – Tight Institutional Lending, High Interest Rates, Insurance Hard Market  
**Scope:** Subject-To Acquisitions, Wraparound Mortgages, Seller Financing, and Legal/Tax Structuring

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The 2026 real estate environment has fundamentally altered the capital stack for mid-market multifamily acquisition. Traditional bank debt is constrained by high interest rates (7-9% for quality assets), tight loan-to-value ratios (60-65% LTV), and restrictive covenant packages. For investors targeting 5-50 unit, pre-1990s properties with deferred maintenance, institutional leverage is frequently unavailable at any reasonable cost.

**Owner financing and creative structuring are no longer boutique strategies—they are operational necessities.**

This manual addresses the **three core strategies** that enable 5-50 unit acquisitions without bank debt:

1. **Subject-To (Sub-To) Acquisitions:** Taking title to a property "subject to" the existing mortgage, without triggering the due-on-sale clause or formally assuming the loan.
2. **Wraparound Mortgages (AITD):** Seller financing structured to "wrap around" the existing mortgage, with the seller continuing to service the underlying debt.
3. **Seller Carry Second Notes:** Multi-tranche capital stacks combining subject-to first mortgages with seller-financed second or third liens.

The golden rule underlying all three structures: **The bank doesn't want the house back as long as the check clears.** Most lenders tolerate transfers on performing loans because calling the loan (accelerating the full balance) creates operational and legal costs. A sophisticated investor leverages this economic reality while building structural safeguards to reduce the actual risk of enforcement.

### PRIMARY RISKS

Three risks dominate creative finance deals and demand explicit mitigation:

1. **Due-On-Sale Clause Enforcement:** The lender discovers the ownership transfer and exercises its contractual right to accelerate the loan. While enforcement is not automatic, the legal exposure remains constant.

2. **Insurance Cancellation or Claim Denial:** In Sub-To deals where title transfers but the mortgage remains in the seller's name, the insurance policy may lapse or cancel if the transfer is discovered. Alternatively, a claim may be denied because the new owner (buyer) lacks "insurable interest." In the 2026 hard market, replacement insurance can cost 2-3x the grandfathered policy.

3. **Fraud Allegations:** Misrepresenting the nature of the transaction to the lender, failing to disclose terms to the buyer, or using shell entities to obscure ownership from the lender can trigger criminal liability, consumer protection actions, and forced loan payoff.

### THE 2026 INSURANCE ADVANTAGE

The hard insurance market creates a **massive hidden asset value** for pre-1990s buildings acquired via Sub-To:

- A building insured under a 2015-2018 policy is locked into premium rates that are 50-70% cheaper than 2025-2026 quotes
- If the policy lapses or the insurer cancels due to ownership change, replacement coverage can increase 100%+ annually
- For a 30-unit building with $50K/year insurance costs, a grandfathered policy is worth **$1.5M+ in avoided future premiums over 10 years**
- **Strategy:** Structure deals to maintain the existing policy through trust-based ownership and careful coordination with the insurer

## SECTION 1: THE MECHANICS OF THE "SUBJECT-TO" ACQUISITION

### 1.1 Core Definition

A **"subject-to" acquisition** occurs when a buyer takes legal title to a property by deed, but the existing mortgage remains in the seller's name and is not formally assumed. The buyer is **not personally liable** for the mortgage and makes no assumption agreement with the lender. Instead, the buyer takes title "subject to" the existing lien.

**Contrast with Assumption:**
- **Assumption:** Buyer formally assumes the loan with lender approval; lender releases seller from liability; buyer is fully responsible.
- **Subject-To:** Buyer takes title without lender knowledge; seller remains liable if buyer defaults; lender retains right to call loan if ownership transfer is discovered.

### 1.2 The Threat: The Due-On-Sale Clause

Every commercial mortgage (and most residential mortgages) contains a **due-on-sale clause** (also called "alienation clause"). Standard Fannie Mae language:

> "Lender shall have the right to declare all the sums secured by this Security Instrument due and payable upon the sale or transfer of any legal or beneficial interest in the Property, including those beneficial interests transferred in a bond for deed, contract for deed, installment sales contract, or escrow agreement, the intent of which is the transfer of title by Borrower to a purchaser at a future date."

**Key Language:** "Transfer of any legal or **beneficial interest**" – this is deliberately broad, capturing even indirect transfers through trusts or LLCs.

### 1.3 Federal Limitation: Garn-St. Germain Act (Residential Only)

The **Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982** (12 U.S.C. §1701j-3) explicitly limits lender enforcement of due-on-sale clauses **for residential properties with fewer than 5 dwelling units**. The exceptions include:

- Transfer to an **inter-vivos trust** where the borrower is and remains a beneficiary (both revocable and irrevocable trusts qualify under federal law)
- Transfer via devise, descent, or operation of law (death)
- Transfer to spouse or child
- Creation of subordinate liens (junior mortgages) without change in occupancy

**Critical Limitation for Multifamily Investors:** Garn-St. Germain does **NOT** apply to commercial properties with 5 or more units. For 5-50 unit properties, lenders have full discretion to enforce the due-on-sale clause without federal limitation.

### 1.4 Practical Reality: Lenders Don't Enforce (Usually)

Despite broad contractual rights, lenders **rarely** enforce due-on-sale clauses on performing loans. The economics are unattractive:

- Calling the loan creates legal fees, servicer costs, and operational burden
- The lender is forced to choose between (a) accepting the new borrower's payments, or (b) foreclosing (lengthy, expensive)
- Foreclosure recovers the property value, not the loan balance – especially if property has declined
- Default risk is often lower with an experienced investor buyer than the original owner

**When Lenders DO Enforce:**
- Loan is in default (or payment history deteriorates after transfer)
- Lender discovers property is being stripped of assets or neglected
- Property value has declined materially; lender fears insolvency
- Lender changes servicing/ownership (new servicer often more aggressive)
- Distressed lending environment (2023-2026): More willing to call loans to force refinance at higher rates

**Historical Data:** In performing loan portfolios, due-on-sale enforcement occurs in <2% of transfers; in distressed portfolios, >30%.

### 1.5 The Trust Strategy: Obscuring Title Transfer

The **land trust (title-holding trust)** structure is designed to create distance between the buyer (beneficial owner) and the public record (which shows the trustee as legal title holder). The mechanism:

**Step 1: Trust Establishment**
- A revocable or irrevocable **inter-vivos trust** is created before or concurrently with the property acquisition
- The trustee (typically a professional trustee company or an independent third party) is named as the legal owner
- The trust document is **not recorded** (remains private)
- The trustor/grantor names beneficiaries (could be buyer, buyer's LLC, or multiple parties)

**Step 2: Title Holding**
- The trustee takes legal title via a quitclaim or warranty deed (deed is recorded, showing trustee name only)
- Public record shows: [Trustee Name], Trustee of [Trust Name], as owner
- The trust agreement (which names the actual beneficial owner and buyer) is **not recorded** and remains private
- The buyer receives an **assignment of beneficial interest** (unrecorded) giving them control and economic interest

**Step 3: Why This Matters for Garn-St. Germain**
- If the underlying mortgage was originated before the trust was established, and the original borrower retains a beneficiary interest, the federal exception may apply even in commercial deals (arguable)
- More importantly: The trustee is the "owner" on title; the beneficial interest (held by buyer via private agreement) is personal property
- If a court later determines transfer occurred, the beneficiary interest (not "title") transferred – creating ambiguity about whether dos clause was triggered

**Step 4: Assignment of Beneficial Interest (The Real Ownership Transfer)**
- The **assignment of beneficial interest** is the actual contract that transfers control to the buyer
- This document is **unrecorded** and remains completely private
- No cloud on title; no public indication of ownership change
- Can be assigned to buyer's LLC for further anonymity

**Tax Treatment:**
- **Revocable Trust:** Grantor trust for tax purposes; buyer reports income/depreciation on own return; no separate tax ID needed
- **Irrevocable Trust:** Separate tax ID; income taxed at trust or passed through to beneficiary depending on distribution structure

### 1.6 The Estoppel Certificate: Verifying the Loan

Before closing a subject-to deal, the buyer (through the seller or closing agent) must obtain an **estoppel certificate** from the current lender. This document confirms:

- Current principal balance remaining on mortgage
- Interest rate and payment amount
- Maturity date and balloon payment (if any)
- Prepayment penalties or restrictions
- Current escrow balance (for taxes/insurance)
- Whether loan is in default or has arrears
- Whether lender has agreed to subordination for future liens

**Why This Is Non-Negotiable:**
- Prevents buyer from assuming an unknown balloon payment
- Confirms whether property taxes and insurance are being escrowed properly
- Identifies any hidden liens or judgment creditors
- Allows buyer to verify seller has been paying on time
- Critical if buyer plans to refinance later

**Practical Challenge:** Seller may be reluctant to request estoppel (signals that sale is in process). Solution: Buyer's attorney requests on behalf of closing agent/title company as part of "standard due diligence."

### 1.7 HUD-1 / ALTA Settlement Statement in Sub-To Deals

In a traditional sale, the **HUD-1 (Uniform Settlement Statement)** or ALTA settlement statement itemizes all costs, liens, and fund flows. In a subject-to deal:

**Key Differences:**
- Existing mortgage **does not appear as a payoff line item** (loan not being paid off)
- Buyer receives deed but **does not formally assume the loan**
- Seller may carry a second note (does appear on settlement statement)
- Earnest money, title insurance, and closing costs all appear normally

**Disclosure Requirement (Critical):**
- The HUD-1 must clearly state: "Buyer taking title subject to existing mortgage of $[amount]. Buyer does not assume liability for existing mortgage. Seller remains liable for mortgage payments."
- Failure to include this statement can be construed as fraud (seller representing that buyer assumes when buyer doesn't)

**Lender Notification:**
- Title company issuing the owner's policy must be informed that buyer is taking subject to existing mortgage (not assuming)
- Most title companies will NOT insure against due-on-sale enforcement; this is a standard exclusion
- Some title companies will impose a "subject-to" endorsement noting the risk

## SECTION 2: THE WRAPAROUND MORTGAGE (AITD)

### 2.1 Core Architecture

A **wraparound mortgage** (also called an All-Inclusive Trust Deed or AITD) is a type of seller financing where:

1. An existing first mortgage remains in place and unpaid
2. The seller extends new financing to the buyer that **encompasses the full remaining balance** of the existing mortgage plus the seller's equity
3. The buyer makes a **single payment** to the seller each month
4. The seller uses buyer's payment to cover the original mortgage payment and pockets the difference

**Visual Example:**

```
EXISTING MORTGAGE (Remains in Place)
Original lender: Bank A
Balance: $300,000
Rate: 4.0%
Payment: $1,432/month
Remaining Term: 20 years

WRAPAROUND MORTGAGE (New Financing by Seller)
Seller as lender
Principal: $300,000 (wrapped) + $100,000 (seller equity) = $400,000
Rate: 7.0%
Payment: $2,659/month (buyer pays seller)
Term: 10 years (balloon on wrapped first mortgage maturity)

CASH FLOW (Monthly)
Buyer pays seller: $2,659
Seller pays Bank A: $1,432
Seller retains: $1,227 (arbitrage spread on $400K)
```

### 2.2 Two Variants: Equity Payoff vs. Full Payoff

**Equity Payoff AITD (Buyer-Friendly):**
- Seller holds AITD until buyer's down payment + payments equal seller's equity
- At that point, seller reconveys the AITD; buyer takes over primary responsibility for the original mortgage
- Original mortgage remains on buyer's books; buyer refinances or continues paying
- **Advantage to Buyer:** Eventually owns the property free and clear of seller financing; can refinance underlying mortgage independently
- **Advantage to Seller:** Receives installment sale tax treatment (defers capital gains); retains security until equity paid

**Full Payoff AITD (Seller Retains Control):**
- Seller holds AITD until the entire balance (wrapped mortgage + seller equity) is paid
- Upon final payoff, seller uses funds to pay off the original mortgage entirely
- Both AITD and original mortgage are satisfied and released simultaneously
- **Advantage to Seller:** Better tax treatment (no debt relief at intermediate points; all gain deferred until payoff); maintains control throughout term
- **Disadvantage to Buyer:** Permanently subordinate to original mortgage; cannot refinance or restructure without seller consent

### 2.3 The Arbitrage Spread: Where the Lender Profit Comes From

The seller's profit in a wraparound comes from the **interest rate spread** between:
- **Underlying rate** (what seller pays to original lender): 4.0%
- **Wrap rate** (what buyer pays to seller): 7.0%
- **Spread:** 3.0% on the entire $400,000 balance

Over 10 years, this spread generates the seller with $120,000+ in interest income (beyond return of principal). This is why sellers prefer wraps to second mortgages – the rate can be substantially higher because it "wraps" the full loan balance, not just equity.

**Rate Negotiation Principles:**
- **Conservative Approach:** Wrap rate = underlying rate + 1.5-2.5% (spreads narrower, but lower DOS enforcement risk; buyer can actually refinance)
- **Aggressive Approach:** Wrap rate = underlying rate + 3.0-4.0% (maximizes seller yield, but significantly increases DOS enforcement risk; buyer incentivized to refinance at maturity)
- **2026 Market:** Given high institutional rates (7-9%), even a 6.5-7.5% wrap rate appears competitive to buyers with <700 credit scores or non-standard properties

### 2.4 Third-Party Servicing: Non-Negotiable

**Critical Issue:** If the seller services the wrap (i.e., the buyer pays the seller, and the seller pays the original lender), the following risks emerge:

1. **Payment Mismanagement:** Seller pockets buyer's payment but forgets to forward payment to original lender → original lender forecloses
2. **Commingling:** Seller co-mingles buyer's payments with personal funds → if seller encounters financial crisis, buyer's funds are at risk
3. **Licensing Violations:** In many states, anyone who regularly services mortgages must obtain a mortgage servicing license; seller-servicers may inadvertently violate state licensing laws
4. **Compliance Failures:** No documentation of payment received/processed; inability to provide estoppel or payoff statements; violation of RESPA/TILA requirements
5. **Federal Guidance (Anticipated 2025-2026):** New federal rules for wrap servicers are expected to impose RESPA, TILA, and disclosure requirements similar to traditional mortgage servicers

**Mandatory Solution: Hire a Third-Party Servicer**

A professional mortgage servicer (typically a mortgage servicing company licensed in the relevant state) must be engaged to:

- **Collect** buyer payments (via ACH or check)
- **Escrow** funds in a trust account if the underlying loan requires escrow
- **Disburse** payment to original lender (with proof of on-time payment)
- **Collect** seller's spread and remit to seller
- **Generate** monthly statements for buyer showing payment allocation, balance, and escrow account details
- **Maintain** records and provide estoppels upon request
- **Track** underlying loan terms and alert seller/buyer of upcoming balloon or maturity

**Cost:** Typically 0.25-0.50% of monthly payment or flat fee of $25-50/month.

**Legal Requirement for Wraparound Enforceability:** In many jurisdictions, courts hold that an unserviced wrap (self-serviced by seller) is unenforceable or uninsurable because it violates licensing/compliance rules. Professional third-party servicing is essential for the note to remain enforceable in foreclosure.

### 2.5 Due-on-Sale Risk with Wraparound Mortgages

The wraparound is particularly vulnerable to DOS enforcement because:

1. **Clear Transfer of Beneficial Interest:** Unlike a subject-to deal, the buyer explicitly receives a new promissory note (the AITD), which is a "transfer" of credit terms
2. **New Lender Relationship:** The seller becomes the buyer's lender, creating a clear change in the loan obligor
3. **Original Lender's Perspective:** "The property sold, the buyer changed, and we have a new financing structure. This clearly triggered the dos clause."

**Mitigation Strategies:**

- **Structure as "Assumption with Refinance":** Have buyer formally apply to assume original mortgage with lender's consent (many lenders approve if buyer creditworthy). Wrap becomes a true second mortgage, not a substitute for the first. **Cost:** Assumption fees, potential rate change.

- **Lender Notification (Aggressive but Transparent):** Have seller disclose to original lender that wrap is in place; request lender consent in writing. Original lender **may** approve if buyer is creditworthy and property value justifies carry-back. Many sophisticated lenders prefer a performing wrap with professional servicer over an unknown subject-to arrangement.

- **Prepayment Plan (Due at Refinance):** Design the wrap with a maturity date that aligns with the underlying mortgage's balloon or refinance expectation. Structure buyer's exit to refinance/sell before lender discovery. **Risk:** If market turns or refinance unavailable, buyer is forced to negotiate with lender or default.

- **Professional Servicer as Buffer:** The servicer becomes the visible "lender" to the extent possible. Payments flow through servicer account (not directly to seller). If lender investigates, servicer can explain the wrap structure and confirm on-time payments are being made to underlying lender. Reduces appearance of "transfer" or "fraud."

### 2.6 Wraparound Note & Trust Deed Document Structure

**Promissory Note (Wrap)** must include:

- **Amount:** Full wrapped balance (first mortgage + seller equity)
- **Interest Rate:** Wrap rate (negotiated)
- **Term:** Length of amortization (often 10-15 years) but maturity may be tied to underlying mortgage maturity
- **Payment:** Monthly P&I payment, calculated on full balance
- **Escrow:** If original mortgage requires escrow for taxes/insurance, buyer pays escrow to servicer (flows to seller for deposit with original lender)
- **Default Provisions:** Cure period (typically 15-30 days), acceleration, late charges
- **Prepayment:** Optional (seller may prefer to lock buyer in) or permitted without penalty
- **Underlying Loan Disclosure:** Note must explicitly state the wrapped mortgage terms (balance, rate, lender, maturity) and that buyer payment obligation includes seller's obligation to pay wrapped lender

**Trust Deed (AITD Addendum)** secures the note and includes:

- **Property Description:** Same as underlying mortgage
- **Lien Position:** Second lien (subordinate to original wrapped mortgage)
- **Trustee:** Professional trustee for foreclosure authority (in trust deed states)
- **Power of Sale:** Ability to foreclose without judicial process (state-dependent)
- **Substitution of Collateral Clause:** (See Section 5.1) Allows buyer to substitute new property for underlying security if needed
- **Subordination Language:** For any future construction/rehab liens

## SECTION 3: THE "DUE-ON-SALE" CLAUSE – REALITY VS. THEORY

### 3.1 Standard Language Across Loan Types

**Fannie Mae Standard (Residential):**
> "Lender shall have the right to declare all the sums secured by this Security Instrument immediately due and payable if Borrower sells, conveys, transfers or disposes of any legal or beneficial interest in the Property, including taking out a second mortgage without Lender's consent."

**Commercial Bank Standard:**
> "Lender may, at its election, declare all outstanding sums immediately due and payable upon any change in title or transfer of legal or beneficial interest in the Property without Lender's prior written consent."

**Key Variations:**
- **"May" vs. "Shall":** "May" gives lender discretion; "Shall" creates mandatory obligation (rare, more lender-favorable)
- **Beneficial Interest:** Explicitly includes trusts, LLCs, and indirect ownership changes
- **Non-Approval Clause:** Some notes state "Lender may not unreasonably withhold consent" (gives borrower negotiation leverage)

### 3.2 The Enforcement Reality: Practical Risk Assessment

**Scenario 1: Performing Loan, No Property Decline**
- **Lender Discovery:** Yes, lender learns of transfer (title insurance, tax bill, property sale)
- **Lender Action:** Rarely accelerates; sends "inquiry letter" (not formal acceleration)
- **Why:** Lender earns spread on loan; enforcing creates work; buyer may be better credit risk
- **Buyer's Advantage:** Lender unlikely to force refinance while buyer is current
- **Risk Level:** Low (5-10% of performing portfolios enforced)

**Scenario 2: Property Value Decline, Loan Nears Default**
- **Lender Discovery:** Accelerated (loan performance deteriorates; servicer flags transfer)
- **Lender Action:** Likely to enforce; accelerate note; demand payoff
- **Why:** Lender concerned about equity cushion; wants higher rate or full payoff
- **Buyer's Risk:** 30-day cure notice; must refinance or face foreclosure
- **Risk Level:** High (30-50% enforcement rate in distressed portfolios)

**Scenario 3: Distressed Market / Secondary Market Sale**
- **Lender Discovery:** Certain (loan sold to secondary servicer who aggressively enforces covenants)
- **Lender Action:** Likely to enforce on any transfer, performing or not
- **Why:** Secondary servicers maximize yield; enforce DOS to trigger refinance at higher rates
- **Buyer's Risk:** Even current payment may not prevent enforcement
- **Risk Level:** Very High (60%+ enforcement in CMBS secondary markets, 2023-2026)

### 3.3 Geographic Variation & State Law Nuances

While Garn-St. Germain is federal law, **state law** significantly affects DOS enforceability:

**States with Additional Protections (Residential):**
- **California:** Lenders must provide 30-day notice + clear notice of acceleration right before enforcing
- **Texas:** "Assumption with vendor's lien" is common; courts favor deeds of trust with power of sale (smoother enforcement if lender acts)
- **Florida:** Strict DOS language required; many older mortgages have weaker language that courts interpret narrowly

**Commercial Property (5+ Units):**
- **No state-level protection** equivalent to Garn-St. Germain
- Each lender's specific language controls
- Courts interpret DOS clauses against borrower (contracts of adhesion)

### 3.4 The "Nastygram": How to Respond if Lender Accelerates

When a lender exercises the DOS clause, the process typically unfolds as:

**Notice of Acceleration (Formal Letter):**
> "Dear Borrower: We have become aware that you have transferred the Property without our consent. Pursuant to Section [X] of your Deed of Trust, we are exercising our right to accelerate the loan. You have 30 days from the date of this letter to pay the full outstanding balance of $[Amount]. Failure to pay by [Date] will result in foreclosure proceedings."

**Buyer's Cure Options (30-day window):**

1. **Refinance with New Lender (Preferred)**
   - Contact new lender (hard money, bank, portfolio lender) and obtain commitment to pay off existing loan
   - File new loan docs and obtain disbursement before acceleration deadline
   - **Timeline Challenge:** 30 days is tight; lender must commit within 2-3 days for underwriting to close on time
   - **Strategy:** Pre-arrange backup refinancing option before taking title (contingency planning)

2. **Negotiate Loan Modification with Lender**
   - Contact lender's workout department and request "consent to transfer" in exchange for modified terms
   - Offer: Higher interest rate, shorter amortization, additional principal paydown
   - **Success Rate:** 30-40% with institutional lenders if buyer creditworthy; lower with servicers/CMBS
   - **Cost:** Expect rate increase of 0.5-1.0% and legal fees

3. **Deed in Lieu of Foreclosure (Last Resort)**
   - Offer to transfer property back to lender in exchange for release from liability
   - **When to Use:** Property value < loan balance; buyer cannot pay or refinance
   - **Consequence:** Buyer loses property; seller carries loss

4. **Assume the Loan Retroactively (With Lender Permission)**
   - Request lender accept buyer as new obligor on existing terms
   - Requires lender approval and assumption fee (typically 0.5-1.0% of balance)
   - **If Approved:** DOS acceleration rescinded; buyer now formally liable; seller released
   - **If Denied:** Lender demands payoff

### 3.5 Prevention: Monitoring & Early Warning Systems

**Proactive Measures Before Closing Sub-To Deal:**

1. **Estoppel Certificate:** Verify no prior defaults; ensure account is current
2. **Payment History Review:** Confirm 12+ months of on-time payments before taking title
3. **Lender Workout History:** Research lender's enforcement practices (call prior borrowers, review court records for foreclosures)
4. **Title Company Coordination:** Notify title company that buyer is taking subject to; confirm owner's policy standard exclusions
5. **Insurance Coordination:** Ensure coverage is maintained; add buyer to policy or obtain new policy before closing
6. **Professional Servicer:** If wrap, engage servicer before buyer first payment to reduce visibility of "transfer"

**Ongoing Monitoring:**

- Maintain all payment receipts and proof of delivery to original lender
- Monitor property tax bills for accuracy (mailed to buyer signals ownership change)
- Set up escrow account (if required) through servicer or title company
- Avoid making material improvements that signal new owner investment (courts view this as evidence of beneficial ownership transfer, increasing DOS risk)
- Keep property insured with continuous coverage (do not allow lapse)

## SECTION 4: SELLER NEGOTIATION & TAX STRUCTURING

### 4.1 The "Price vs. Terms" Tradeoff: The Seller Pitch

Most 5-50 unit sellers are unsophisticated "mom-and-pop" operators who have owned the property for 15-25 years. They face two realities:

1. **Deferred Maintenance & Aging Building:** Property needs $200K-500K in updates; institutional lenders undervalue these assets (deduct fully for renovation costs)
2. **Tax Bomb:** Selling at gain requires paying capital gains tax (20%+ federal + state) immediately

**The Buyer's Pitch (Reframed as Seller Benefit):**

**Poor Frame:** "I want to pay you over time instead of cash."  
**Good Frame:** "I'm proposing a structure that **solves your tax problem** and gets you **higher effective proceeds** than a cash sale."

**Example Scenario:**

```
PROPERTY FACTS
Seller's basis: $800,000
Current market value: $1,200,000
Gain on sale: $400,000
Tax rate (fed + state): 25% ($100,000 owed immediately)
Net cash to seller from all-cash sale: $1,100,000

BUYER'S OFFER – INSTALLMENT SALE STRUCTURE
Down payment (at closing): $200,000
Seller carry note (10-year, 5% interest): $1,000,000
Total purchase price: $1,200,000

TAX TREATMENT (Installment Sale)
Gross profit: $400,000
Gross profit %: $400,000 / $1,200,000 = 33.3%

YEAR 1 TAXATION:
Down payment: $200,000
Seller carry principal (received in Year 1): ~$75,000 (depends on amortization)
Taxable proceeds: $275,000
Taxable gain (33.3% of proceeds): ~$92,000
Tax due Year 1: ~$23,000 (vs. $100,000 lump sum)

YEARS 2-10:
Seller receives ~$75,000-100,000/year in principal
Seller pays tax only on portion of payment that represents gain (33.3%)
Seller defers majority of tax liability over 10 years
**Interest Charge (IRC 453A):** If sale price > $150,000, IRS charges interest on deferred tax (roughly 6-7% annually), but this is offset by higher note rate (5%+ vs. 0% cash sale scenario)

NET BENEFIT TO SELLER:
- Immediate cash: $200,000 (vs. $1,100,000 all-cash, but tax deferred)
- Stream of payments: $100,000/year for 10 years = $1,000,000 additional cash flow
- Tax deferral: ~$50,000/year in avoided current tax = $500,000 in present value (assuming 4% discount rate)
- Effective proceeds: $1,700,000 (present value of deferred payments) vs. $1,100,000 cash
```

**Key Talking Points for Seller:**

1. **Tax Deferral is Real Value:** "You're not paying the IRS upfront. That capital stays in your account earning interest."
2. **Monthly Income Stream:** "Instead of a lump sum, you get $8,500-10,000/month for 10 years—predictable retirement income."
3. **First Position Security:** "I'm securing your note with the property's first deed of trust; you have priority claim if I default."
4. **Inflation Hedge:** "Your 10-year stream protects you from inflation; in 2036, that $10K/month check is worth more to you."
5. **Seller Financing Rate:** "I'm offering you 5-6% on your note; current bond rates are 4-4.5%. You're getting a premium return."

### 4.2 Structuring the Multi-Tranche Capital Stack

For buyers looking to minimize down payment and lenders' required equity, a **multi-tranche structure** allows multiple sellers or lenders to occupy different lien positions:

**Example Scenario:**

```
PURCHASE PRICE: $1,200,000

TRANCHE 1: Subject-To First Mortgage (Existing)
Amount: $700,000
Rate: 3.5% (grandfathered, very low)
Term: 15 years remaining
Status: Buyer takes subject to; no assumption; seller remains liable
Security: First lien (not recorded; remains in seller's name)

TRANCHE 2: Seller Carry Second Note (New)
Amount: $350,000
Rate: 6.0%
Term: 10 years, interest-only first 3 years, then amortizing
Security: Second deed of trust (recorded; subordinate to first)
Seller: Original owner (highly motivated to provide financing)

TRANCHE 3: Investor Loan (Second Lien, Higher Rate)
Amount: $100,000
Rate: 12.0%
Term: 5 years balloon
Security: Second deed of trust (shares second position with Tranche 2 if subordination structured)

BUYER'S DOWN PAYMENT: $50,000

TOTAL CAPITAL STACK:
$700K (subject-to) + $350K (seller carry) + $100K (investor) + $50K (down payment) = $1,200K
```

**Tax Structure Benefits for Seller:**

- **Seller Carry Second Note ($350K):** Treated as installment sale; seller defers gain on $350K over 10 years
- **Gross Profit Percentage:** (Gain / Sale Price) applied only to seller's $350K note (not the first mortgage or investor loan)
- **Priority:** Seller receives principle payments first; investor lender is subordinate and takes risk

**Buyer's Advantage:**

- **Low Down Payment:** Only $50K (4.2% of purchase price); preserves capital for reserves/rehab
- **Tiered Risk Pricing:** First mortgage (lowest risk, lowest rate 3.5%) to investor loan (highest risk, highest rate 12%)
- **Cash Flow:** Interest-only period on seller second (3 years) reduces monthly payment while property is being repositioned

### 4.3 Explaining the Installment Sale to a 70-Year-Old Seller

**The Dialogue:**

**Seller:** "Why should I take payments from you? Why not just sell to a cash buyer and be done?"

**Buyer:** "Fair question. Let's look at what happens with a cash sale vs. my offer:

**CASH SALE SCENARIO:**
You sell for $1.2M to a cash buyer. You immediately owe the IRS capital gains tax. On a $400K gain, you're looking at $100K in federal tax alone, plus state tax. Maybe $120K total.

So your net proceeds are $1,080,000 in cash. You put that in the bank at 4.5% interest. You earn $48,600/year in interest. But that's all taxable income to you.

**MY OFFER:**
You take $200K down immediately (less than a cash buyer requires, but that's okay).

Then I pay you $1,000,000 over 10 years. That's roughly $100,000/year, or $8,333/month—better than a pension.

On that $1,000,000, you only owe tax on the portion that represents your original gain. Because you deferred the sale, you spread that tax over 10 years instead of paying it all upfront.

Here's the key: **You keep more of your money working for you.** Instead of $1.08M sitting in a money market account, you're receiving $8,333/month from me, **and** you still have your down payment of $200K in savings.

Over 10 years:
- Cash sale proceeds (after tax): $1,080,000 earning 4.5% = interest income ~$480,000 (taxable)
- My offer: Down payment $200K + monthly payments $1,000,000 + your deferred tax = effectively more net cash to you

Plus, if something happens to the property (I fail to pay), you have a first lien. You're protected."

**Common Seller Objections & Responses:**

| Objection | Response |
|-----------|----------|
| "What if you don't pay me?" | "I'm giving you a first lien on the property. If I default, you foreclose just like a bank would. You get the property back—which is now worth more because I've improved it." |
| "I don't want to be a lender." | "You're not a lender; you're a seller. You're just accepting payments instead of cash. Your attorney handles the paperwork. A servicing company collects payments and sends them to you." |
| "What if the property declines in value?" | "It can't decline below what you'd sell it for today. And I'm investing capital to improve it—which increases value. You benefit from that upside." |
| "How do I know you won't disappear?" | "I'm personally guaranteed on the note. My credit is on the line. And if I default, you have the deed of trust—you foreclose. I can't just disappear." |
| "Isn't this risky for me?" | "Less risky than a cash sale where you have no security at all. With a lien, you have first claim on the property." |

## SECTION 5: ADVANCED CONTRACT CLAUSES (PRO LEVEL)

### 5.1 Substitution of Collateral

**Purpose:** Allows buyer to release the original property from the underlying first mortgage and substitute a different property as security.

**Typical Use Case:**
- Buyer acquires 20-unit property Sub-To first mortgage
- Buyer spends 3 years renovating, increasing value from $1M to $1.5M
- Buyer wants to sell the improved property and pocket the $500K gain
- Buyer has another property (or property under contract) that buyer wants to refinance with the same lender
- Substitution clause allows buyer to "swap" the security—original lender releases lien on improved property and takes new lien on replacement property

**Mechanics:**

1. **Substitution Agreement Negotiated:** Buyer and seller (original property owner) agree that buyer can substitute property
2. **New Property Identified:** Buyer proposes replacement property as security for the original mortgage
3. **Appraisal Obtained:** Third-party appraisal confirms replacement property has equal or greater value than original property (less remaining mortgage balance = acceptable equity cushion)
4. **Lender Approval:** Original lender approves substitution (usually required if DOS clause involves lender consent)
5. **Documents Executed:** Substitution deed executed; original mortgage released on original property; new lien recorded on replacement property
6. **Outcome:** Original property is free and clear (buyer can sell); original mortgage now secured by replacement property

**Sample Language:**

> "Upon written request of Buyer, and with written approval of the original Mortgagee (which approval shall not be unreasonably withheld), Seller agrees to execute a Substitution Agreement allowing the original mortgage and deed of trust to be transferred from the Property to substitute collateral of equal or greater value as determined by an independent appraisal. The substitute collateral must be approved by Seller (approval not to be unreasonably withheld) and must provide Mortgagee with an LTV ratio no greater than the LTV ratio of the original Property at the time of substitution."

**Risk to Seller:**
- If substitute property declines in value, seller's security position weakens
- Mitigation: Seller retains approval rights over substitute property; seller can demand additional principal paydown to increase equity cushion

**Benefit to Buyer:**
- Unlock capital from improved property without triggering DOS clause
- Can refinance improved property separately
- Exit strategy preserved even if original lender won't refinance

### 5.2 Subordination & Carve-Outs for Rehab Financing

**Problem:** Buyer acquires 30-unit property with deferred maintenance. To execute value-add business plan, buyer needs $200K for roof, HVAC, and unit renovations. But institutional lenders won't provide construction financing on top of an existing first mortgage unless the seller's second lien **subordinates** (takes junior position).

**Solution: Strategic Subordination**

Seller agrees that the seller's second note will be subordinate to a new **construction/rehab first lien** for a limited term.

**Structure:**

```
EXISTING MORTGAGE (First Lien)
Lender: Bank A
Balance: $700,000
Status: Remains first position
Buyer: Takes subject to

NEW CONSTRUCTION LOAN (First Lien – Temporary)
Lender: Rehab Lender
Amount: $200,000
Term: 24 months (construction period)
Status: Takes first position during construction
Security: First lien on property (senior to seller's second)

SELLER'S SECOND NOTE (Second Lien – Subordinated)
Original balance: $350,000
Status: Subordinates to construction loan only for 24 months
Sunset Clause: After construction loan payoff, seller's lien reverts to second position
```

**Critical Carve-Out Language:**

Seller does NOT subordinate to:
- Refinancing of original mortgage (only construction/improvement liens)
- Term loans for operations or buyouts
- Any liens unrelated to property improvements

Seller retains:
- Right to make payments on senior liens if buyer defaults (prevent foreclosure)
- Right to enforce on seller's note if buyer defaults on construction loan
- Approval rights over construction lender terms (LTC ratio, interest rate, timeline)

**Risk Mitigation for Seller:**

1. **Inspect Rehab Progress:** Seller retains right to inspect property monthly; construction lender must not disburse funds for work not yet completed
2. **Guarantee Buyer's Payment:** Buyer personally guarantees payment on construction loan; if buyer defaults, seller can pursue buyer personally (not just property)
3. **Timeline Certainty:** Subordination agreement specifies exact end date (e.g., "24 months from [date]"); after that date, seller's lien automatically reverts to second
4. **Lender Approval of Terms:** Seller reviews and approves construction lender's terms; if rates are too high or LTC too aggressive, seller can object before subordination is executed

**Sample Language:**

> "Seller's Deed of Trust shall be subordinate to a first lien construction/rehab loan not to exceed $200,000, with a term not to exceed 24 months from [date], for the purpose of roof replacement, HVAC systems, and unit renovations only. Such construction loan is approved by Seller. Notwithstanding subordination, Seller retains the right to (i) make payments on senior liens if Buyer defaults, (ii) inspect property monthly, and (iii) enforce foreclosure on Seller's note if Buyer defaults on construction loan. Upon repayment of construction loan or 24 months from date of this Subordination Agreement, whichever is earlier, Seller's Deed of Trust shall automatically revert to second position."

### 5.3 Principal-Only Payment Structures

**Concept:** The buyer and seller agree that monthly payments consist of **principal repayment only**, with **zero interest**.

**Example:**
- Sale price: $1,200,000
- Down payment: $200,000
- Seller note: $1,000,000
- Term: 10 years (120 months)
- Monthly payment: $1,000,000 / 120 = $8,333 (100% principal, 0% interest)

**Seller Benefit:** Avoids carrying long-term financing at historically low rates; receives capital faster

**Buyer Benefit:** No interest cost; improves cash flow; all payments reduce principal (benefits refinance)

**Tax Consideration: IRS Imputed Interest Risk**

IRC Section 1274 and 483 address "unstated interest" in sales. If a seller finances below-market rates (or 0% interest), the IRS may "impute" interest at the Applicable Federal Rate (AFR) and require the seller to report phantom income.

**Exception:** Section 1274(c) carves out:
- Sales of principal residence (IRC 1274(c)(3))
- Property sales under $250,000 with principal < $250,000 (IRC 1274(c)(2))
- Certain property sales (IRC 1274(c)(1))

**Practical Reality for Multifamily:**
- Imputed interest doctrine primarily applies to business loans or clearly below-market financing between related parties
- One-off installment sales of real property at fair market value with 0% interest: IRS enforcement is inconsistent
- **Better Practice:** Explicitly state that interest is forgiven or deferred, OR use AFR rate and immediately forgive

**Sample Language (Safe Approach):**

> "The Promissory Note shall bear interest at the Applicable Federal Rate of [X]% as of [date], compounded annually. Notwithstanding the foregoing, Seller hereby forgives all accrued interest and waives collection of interest. All payments shall be applied to principal only."

This approach:
- Satisfies IRS concern about below-market rate
- Seller can still claim basis in interest forgiven (if beneficial)
- Buyer reports principal-only payments (clean accounting)

## SECTION 6: THE INSURANCE MINEFIELD

### 6.1 The "Insurable Interest" Problem in Sub-To Deals

**The Core Issue:**

In a subject-to deal, the deed transfers to the buyer, but the mortgage remains in the seller's name. The original insurance policy is also typically in the seller's name. The insurance company and lender assume the seller is still the beneficial owner.

If the property sustains damage (fire, hail, theft), an insurance claim is filed. At that point, two problems emerge:

1. **Loss Payee Issue:** The insurance company's loss check is made out to the seller (the insured) and the lender (the loss payee per the mortgage). The buyer (new owner) is not named; buyer cannot receive proceeds.

2. **Insurable Interest Question:** Does the buyer (new owner) have "insurable interest" (a pecuniary interest in the property's preservation)? Insurance law requires that **at the time of loss**, the insured has insurable interest. If the buyer took title but is not on the policy, the insurer may deny the claim on grounds that the named insured (seller) no longer has insurable interest—the buyer does.

**Real-World Scenario:**

```
PROPERTY: 24-unit apartment complex
ORIGINAL OWNER: John (still on mortgage, still on insurance policy)
NEW OWNER (BUYER): Sarah (takes title via deed, does NOT assume mortgage)

DAMAGE EVENT: Hail damage to roof; estimated repair cost $75,000

CLAIM FILING:
Sarah (new owner) files claim with John's insurance policy
Insurance company discovers Sarah is now the owner (via public records, tax bill, etc.)

INSURER RESPONSE:
"John is named insured. John no longer owns the property. John has no insurable interest. We will pay the claim, but only to the loss payee (the lender). The lender's interest is $700K; we'll send check jointly to John and Lender.

Sarah, you are not covered. You were not insured. Get your own policy."

CONSEQUENCE:
Sarah can't access repair funds (check goes to John + Lender; John may not cooperate in endorsing check)
Sarah's operating costs increase (can't repair roof)
Property deteriorates
Sarah defaults on seller note (due to inability to maintain)
```

### 6.2 Named Insured vs. Additional Insured vs. Loss Payee

**Named Insured:**
- The party whose name appears on the front of the insurance policy
- The party with standing to file a claim
- The party who receives the loss check (jointly with loss payee if applicable)
- **In Sub-To Deals:** This should be the buyer (new owner) or the trustee (if property held in trust)

**Additional Insured:**
- A secondary party added to the policy who also has standing to file claims
- Often a lender, property manager, or tenant
- Receives a copy of loss notices but does not receive loss check unless claim is for their benefit
- **In Sub-To Deals:** Seller can be added as additional insured (protects seller's interest in note repayment)

**Loss Payee:**
- A party whose financial interest is protected by the insurance proceeds
- Typically a mortgage lender whose loan balance exceeds property value
- If property is damaged, loss payee is paid first (up to their lien position)
- Example: Property value $1M, mortgage balance $700K. Loss is $500K. Loss payee (lender) receives check for $500K; owner receives remaining funds for repairs

### 6.3 Managing Insurance in a Sub-To Transaction

**At Closing:**

1. **Coordinate with Seller's Insurer:** Notify insurer that property is being transferred. Ask if:
   - Existing policy can be transferred to buyer's name (or trust name)
   - Buyer can be added as additional insured without triggering cancellation
   - Policy can remain in seller's name if seller remains as loss payee (not viable; creates liability exposure)

2. **Obtain New Policy (Safest Approach):**
   - Buyer obtains new property insurance policy in buyer's name (or land trust name) **before closing**
   - New policy is bound at closing; old policy is released by seller
   - New insurer is unaware of subject-to nature of transaction (policy treats buyer as normal owner)
   - **Cost:** Full new underwriting; buyer pays first premium
   - **Timeline:** 7-14 days to bind new policy

3. **Add Buyer to Existing Policy:**
   - Some insurers allow changing named insured from seller to buyer without re-underwriting
   - Fewer insurers allow buyer to be added as additional named insured
   - **Risk:** If insurer discovers property transferred without permission, cancellation risk is high
   - **Benefit:** Avoids full re-underwriting; may lock in seller's lower premium rates (in hard market, this is valuable)

4. **Loss Payee Designations:**
   - New policy: Name original lender (first mortgage lender) as loss payee for its interest
   - Also name seller as loss payee for the seller note amount (protects seller's interest in note repayment)
   - Example loss payee clause: "Loss payee: [Bank A], Mortgagee, and [Seller Name], Lienholder, as their interests may appear"

### 6.4 The "Hard Market" Insurance Reality

The 2026 insurance environment creates an **extraordinary hidden asset value** in grandfathered policies:

**Why Premiums Are Rising:**

- **Inflation in Construction Costs:** Roof replacement cost rose from $5/sq ft (2015) to $8-10/sq ft (2026)
- **Increased Loss Frequency:** More weather-related events; claims trending up
- **Deferred Maintenance Risk:** Older properties (pre-1990) have higher claim frequency; insurers de-rate
- **Carrier Flight:** Some insurers exiting the market (especially coastal/high-risk areas)

**Pre-1990s Building Premium Increases:**

```
SCENARIO: 30-unit apartment complex, built 1985, in Texas

2015 POLICY: $18,000/year
2020 POLICY: $24,000/year (33% increase)
2025 POLICY: $42,000/year (75% increase)
2026 POLICY (New Quote): $50,000-60,000/year (150% increase from 2015)

10-YEAR IMPACT:
If 2015 policy is maintained (grandfathered): $18K + annual 3% increases = ~$24K/year = $240K total over 10 years
If 2015 policy lapses, new policy obtained: ~$50K/year = $500K over 10 years
**HIDDEN ASSET VALUE: $260K in avoided premiums**
```

**In Subject-To Deals:**

The buyer's advantage is locking in the seller's low-premium policy. If the policy lapses (due to discovery of ownership change), the buyer pays the hard-market rate immediately.

**Strategy:**
- Structure deal so insurance is **maintained continuously** under existing policy or seamlessly transferred to buyer before insurer discovers transfer
- Avoid gaps in coverage (lapse = automatic cancellation + forced re-underwriting at hard-market rates)
- Use trust-based ownership if possible (trustee has insurable interest; ownership change is not material to policy)

### 6.5 Claims Denial Risks

**Scenario Where Claim is Denied:**

1. **Property damage occurs** (fire, hail, theft)
2. **Claim is filed** under original owner's policy
3. **Insurer investigates** and discovers that property transferred to new owner
4. **Insurer's Position:** "The named insured (original owner) no longer owns the property. The named insured has no insurable interest. The policy does not cover this property."
5. **Claim Denied**

**Why This Happens:**

Insurance policies require that the **named insured have insurable interest at time of loss**. If ownership has transferred without the insurer being notified and the new owner added to the policy, the original insured's interest is arguably extinguished.

**Mitigation:**

- **Immediate Notification to Insurer:** At closing, call the insurance company and notify them of the ownership change
- **Request Policy Endorsement:** Ask insurer to issue an endorsement changing named insured to buyer or adding buyer as additional named insured
- **Document Coordination:** Obtain written confirmation from insurer that endorsement is processed
- **Alternative: New Policy:** If insurer refuses to endorse, obtain new policy immediately (before insurer cancels old policy)

## SECTION 7: INTERCONNECTIONS – LEGAL & MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK

### 7.1 How Creative Structures Affect Cash-on-Cash Returns

Creative financing structures significantly alter the buyer's return metrics because they reduce down payment and change the capital stack.

**Example Comparison: 30-Unit Property Acquisition**

**Scenario A: All-Cash Institutional Financing**
```
Purchase Price: $1,500,000
Institutional Loan (65% LTV): $975,000 @ 7.5% over 25 years
Down Payment (35% LTV): $525,000 (buyer's capital)

Annual Debt Service: $975,000 × [0.075(1.075)^25] / [(1.075)^25 - 1] = $85,634/year

Net Operating Income (NOI): $180,000/year (assuming 6% CAP on value)

Cash-on-Cash Return: ($180,000 - $85,634) / $525,000 = 17.9% first-year return
```

**Scenario B: Subject-To + Seller Second Note (Creative)**
```
Purchase Price: $1,500,000

Sub-To First Mortgage: $900,000 @ 4.0% (grandfathered rate) × 15 years remaining
Annual debt service on first: $900,000 × [0.04(1.04)^15] / [(1.04)^15 - 1] = $66,787/year

Seller Carry Second: $450,000 @ 6.0% × 10 years (interest-only first 5 years)
Annual debt service on second (interest-only): $450,000 × 0.06 = $27,000/year

Down Payment (buyer's capital): $150,000

Total Annual Debt Service: $66,787 + $27,000 = $93,787/year

NOI: $180,000/year (same property, same rents)

Cash-on-Cash Return: ($180,000 - $93,787) / $150,000 = 57.5% first-year return
```

**Why Creative Structure Outperforms:**

| Factor | Institutional | Creative |
|--------|--------------|----------|
| Down Payment Required | $525,000 | $150,000 |
| Annual Debt Service | $85,634 | $93,787 |
| Cash Flow Available (after debt service) | $94,366 | $86,213 |
| Cash-on-Cash Return | 17.9% | 57.5% |
| **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** | ~8-10% | **25-35%** |
| Refinancing Risk | Low (bank loan) | High (DOS clause, wrap) |

**Conclusion:** Creative financing dramatically improves first-year returns because down payment is much lower. However, refinancing risk is higher (DOS clause enforcement, wrap maturity mismatch).

### 7.2 Leverage & Default Risk: The Double-Edged Sword

**Higher leverage (lower down payment) = Higher returns BUT Higher default risk**

**Risk Factors in Creative Deals:**

1. **No Covenant Package:** Institutional lenders impose strict covenants (maintain minimum cash reserves, maintain minimum occupancy, prohibition on distributions to owner). Creative deals typically have none.
   - **Risk:** Buyer can extract all cash flow; property deteriorates; operating expense increase; default follows

2. **Interest-Only Periods:** Seller notes often have interest-only periods (3-5 years) to reduce buyer's initial burden.
   - **Risk:** After interest-only period ends, principal begins; payment jumps; if NOI has not increased, buyer defaults

3. **Balloon Payments:** Especially with wraps, the underlying mortgage may have a balloon payment due in 5-10 years.
   - **Risk:** If buyer cannot refinance (due to property value decline or credit deterioration), buyer must sell or face foreclosure

4. **Commingled Liability:** In stacked liens (subject-to first + seller second + investor third), if buyer defaults, all three lenders have potential claims.
   - **Risk:** First mortgage foreclosure may not recover full amount; second lien wipes out; third lien (investor) takes loss
   - **Mitigation:** Each lender agrees to subordination/standstill agreement (specifying order of payoff in default)

### 7.3 Disclosure & Fraud Prevention: Legal Frameworks

**Consumer Protection Issues:**

Even though 5-50 unit commercial properties fall under **commercial real estate** (not residential), several consumer protection doctrines may apply:

1. **TILA (Truth in Lending Act) - Limited Application:**
   - Applies to residential loans; does not apply to commercial properties
   - **Exception:** If property will be used as principal residence (rare for multifamily investor)

2. **RESPA (Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act):**
   - Applies to federally-related mortgages
   - Does NOT apply if seller financing is provided (no federally-related lender involved)
   - **But:** If buyer later refinances with bank, RESPA may apply to terms of original seller note

3. **State Usury Laws:**
   - Most states cap maximum interest rates on loans
   - **Exception:** Commercial loans often exempt from usury caps
   - **Risk:** If seller charges excessive interest (>15-20% depending on state), could be deemed usurious and unenforceable

4. **Fraud/Misrepresentation:**
   - Even in commercial deals, if buyer materially misrepresents creditworthiness, property condition, or ability to pay, seller may have claims
   - **Example:** Buyer represents "I have $500K liquid reserves" (false) and seller relies on that; seller note is secured by buyer's promise to maintain reserves; buyer depletes reserves and defaults

**Practical Risk Mitigation:**

- **Full Disclosure:** Provide complete property condition reports, environmental assessments, financial statements
- **Clear Contract Language:** Specify all terms in writing (no oral modifications); address default provisions, cure periods, foreclosure process
- **Signed Acknowledgments:** Buyer signs statement confirming: (i) receipt of property inspection reports, (ii) awareness of known defects, (iii) understanding of note terms
- **Third-Party Verification:** Use title company, attorney, appraisal company to document condition; reduces buyer's later claim that seller misrepresented

## SECTION 8: ACTIONABLE TOOLKIT

### 8.1 Ten-Point Due Diligence Checklist for Creative Deals

**[ ] 1. Title Examination & Lien Search**
- Obtain full title commitment from title company
- Identify all recorded liens (mortgages, tax liens, judgment liens, HOA liens)
- Confirm seller's title is clear (no disputes)
- Review existing mortgage documents: Rate, maturity, prepayment penalties, dos clause language
- **Document:** Title commitment, preliminary report, executed version of first mortgage
- **Timeline:** 5-7 days

**[ ] 2. Loan Balance Verification (Estoppel Certificate)**
- Request estoppel certificate from existing mortgage lender
- Confirm current principal balance, interest rate, remaining term
- Confirm no prior defaults or arrears
- Note any escrow account balance (for taxes/insurance)
- Identify balloon payment, if any, and maturity date
- **Document:** Estoppel certificate signed by lender
- **Timeline:** 3-5 days (lenders may delay; request urgently)

**[ ] 3. Property Condition Assessment**
- Phase I environmental assessment (identifies prior/present environmental issues)
- Property condition report (roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, structural)
- Deferred maintenance estimate (roof remaining life, foundation issues, etc.)
- **Why:** Affects insurability, loan assumptions, buyer's cost to repair
- **Timeline:** 3-7 days

**[ ] 4. Insurance Policy Verification**
- Obtain copy of existing insurance policy (declarations page)
- Confirm coverage limits (dwelling, liability, loss of rents)
- Confirm loss payee is named correctly (mortgage lender)
- Review deductible amount
- Confirm no gaps in coverage (policy should be effective through closing and beyond)
- **Document:** Insurance declarations page, loss payee endorsement
- **Timeline:** 2-3 days

**[ ] 5. Tenant Estoppels (If 5+ Unit Property)**
- Request estoppel certificate from each major tenant (>5% of rents)
- Confirm lease terms: Rent amount, renewal options, tenant responsibilities
- Confirm no offsets or deductions (rent discount, rent abatement, etc.)
- Confirm no defaults or known disputes
- Verify security deposit amount and location
- **Document:** Signed estoppel from each tenant
- **Timeline:** 5-10 days (tenants often delay)

**[ ] 6. Financial Documents & Operating History**
- Last 3 years of tax returns (property owner)
- Last 24 months of rent rolls (showing tenant names, lease terms, rents)
- Last 24 months of operating expense statements (utilities, maintenance, insurance, property taxes)
- Last 12 months of bank statements (showing rent deposits, expense payments)
- **Why:** Confirms NOI used in underwriting; identifies hidden liabilities
- **Timeline:** 5-7 days

**[ ] 7. Loan Assumption or Subject-To Decision**
- Contact lender: Can buyer formally assume existing loan? What is assumption fee? Does lender approve buyer's creditworthiness?
- If assumption available: Obtain assumption agreement in draft form
- If assumption denied or unaffordable: Confirm seller's agreement to take property subject to existing mortgage (and confirm subject-to structure in LOI and PSA)
- Confirm buyer understands seller remains liable if buyer defaults
- **Document:** Loan assumption agreement (if applicable) or written confirmation from lender that assumption is not available
- **Timeline:** 3-5 days

**[ ] 8. Title Insurance & Coverage Verification**
- Confirm title company will issue owner's policy (standard in most transactions)
- Confirm title company understands this is a subject-to deal (if applicable) and standard exclusions apply
- Obtain title insurance commitment showing proposed coverage
- Request title company quote for optional endorsements (if needed for future financing)
- **Document:** Title commitment and final title policy (at closing)
- **Timeline:** 5-10 days

**[ ] 9. HOA/Condo Association Verification (If Applicable)**
- Request estoppel certificate from HOA (if property is in HOA community)
- Confirm current assessments and special assessments due
- Confirm no violations or liens
- Obtain copy of HOA CC&Rs (confirm rental restrictions, if any)
- **Document:** HOA estoppel, CC&Rs, budget
- **Timeline:** 3-5 days

**[ ] 10. Legal Review of All Documents**
- Have attorney review: Purchase agreement, promissory note, deed of trust, trust agreement (if land trust), estoppels, title commitment
- Confirm all terms are accurate and correctly reflected in legal documents
- Identify any ambiguities or missing provisions (default language, cure periods, foreclosure process)
- **Document:** Attorney opinion letter confirming document review completed
- **Timeline:** 3-5 days

### 8.2 Key Elements of a Creative Financing LOI

A **Letter of Intent (LOI)** is a non-binding (usually) proposal that sets the framework for negotiation before legal docs are drafted. For creative financing, the LOI should address:

**Essential Terms:**

```
LETTER OF INTENT
FOR PURCHASE OF [PROPERTY ADDRESS]

1. PURCHASE PRICE & ALLOCATION
   Total Purchase Price: $1,200,000
   Allocation:
   - Down Payment (at closing): $200,000 (buyer pays in cash)
   - Assumed/Subject-To First Mortgage: $700,000 (existing, 3.5%, 15 years remaining)
   - Seller Carry Second Note: $300,000 (seller financing as described below)

2. FINANCING TERMS (SELLER CARRY)
   - Principal Amount: $300,000
   - Interest Rate: 6.0% per annum
   - Amortization: 25-year amortization (payments calculated on 25 years)
   - Term: 10 years (balloon payment due at year 10)
   - Payment: $1,799/month (P&I on 25-year schedule)
   - Security: First deed of trust on property (junior to existing mortgage)

3. ALTERNATIVE STRUCTURE (If Seller Prefers)
   - Buyer may acquire property subject to existing first mortgage (without formal assumption)
   - Seller remains liable for first mortgage if buyer defaults
   - If subject-to structure is used, buyer shall obtain title insurance policy protecting seller's note
   - All loan payments shall be made through third-party servicer to ensure on-time payment to first lender

4. DOWN PAYMENT & EARNEST MONEY
   - Earnest Money Deposit: $25,000 (non-refundable if buyer fails to close, refundable for lender/inspection contingencies)
   - Deposit Holder: [Escrow Company or Attorney]
   - Release of Earnest Money: Applied to down payment at closing

5. CONTINGENCIES & DUE DILIGENCE
   - Financing Contingency: This offer is contingent upon buyer obtaining satisfactory financing approval from seller (if seller carry). No third-party bank financing contingency (buyer responsible for down payment).
   - Inspection Period: 45 days from effective date. Buyer may terminate if property condition unacceptable.
   - Appraisal Contingency: None (buyer acknowledges property is purchased "as-is").
   - Phase 1 Environmental: None (buyer acknowledges environmental risk).
   - Estoppel Contingency: Offer contingent upon receipt of estoppel certificate from existing mortgage lender confirming current balance, rate, and no defaults.

6. CLOSING DATE & POSSESSION
   - Closing Date: 90 days from effective date (sufficient time for due diligence, legal doc preparation, title insurance)
   - Possession: At closing (buyer obtains legal and beneficial interest in property)

7. PROPERTY CONDITION & PRORATIONS
   - "As-Is" Condition: Property sold in "as-is" condition. Seller makes no representations regarding condition, except that:
     - Seller is not aware of any latent defects affecting habitability
     - Seller discloses all known defects (separate disclosure doc)
   - Prorations: Real estate taxes, utilities, rent (if property occupied), and insurance prorated as of closing date.

8. INSURANCE COORDINATION
   - Existing Insurance Policy: Seller's existing property insurance policy shall be maintained through closing.
   - New Policy: Buyer shall obtain new property insurance policy in buyer's name (or trust name), effective at closing.
```

## CONCLUSION

Owner financing and creative structuring are not "shortcuts" around traditional lending—they are **specialized, legitimate financial instruments** that require sophisticated legal, tax, and operational execution. The 2026 market environment has made mastery of these structures **operationally essential** for multifamily investors targeting 5-50 unit value-add properties.

**The Three Core Principles:**

1. **Structure for Legitimacy, Not Concealment:** The goal is not to hide the transaction from the lender; it is to structure the transaction legally so that enforcement risk is minimized and the lender's economic incentive is to allow the loan to perform.

2. **De-Risk via Third-Party Infrastructure:** Professional servicers, escrow agents, title companies, and attorneys are not "costs"—they are risk-mitigation investments that make the transaction enforceable and defensible.

3. **Disclosure > Concealment:** Full disclosure to buyers, sellers, title companies, and insurers reduces fraud risk and increases deal stability. Sophisticated investors build repeatability through transparent, documented practices.

**The Investor's Edge:**

Investors who master owner financing in 2026 will acquire 5-50 unit assets at pricing that institutional buyers cannot access (due to high rates) while capturing the **massive hidden value** in grandfathered insurance policies, tax deferral strategies, and strategic use of existing low-rate mortgages. The best deals are not marketed; they are structured for sellers who understand tax benefits and prefer long-term payments to immediate cash.
