# Legal Frameworks & Transaction Compliance for 5-50 Unit Multifamily Asset Investing

## Executive Summary

The 5-50 unit multifamily space presents unique legal and transactional challenges absent from larger institutional portfolios or single-family markets. Standardized commercial contracts—designed for trophy-class assets or sophisticated operators—create dangerous gaps when applied to pre-1990s buildings with unsophisticated "mom and pop" sellers, deteriorated systems (aluminum wiring, polybutylene), and murky tenant documentation. This report synthesizes the legal backbone required to navigate these risks while maintaining deal flow from sellers who lack sophisticated legal counsel.

**The three critical legal risks in 2026 are:** (1) **Insurance bindability collapse** forcing renegotiation on properties flagged for code-era defects, (2) **Rent Control exposure** (especially CA/NY) that can reduce projected value-add NOI capture by 30-50% if underwriting ignores protected-tenant classes, and (3) **Title defects from unrecorded easements and long-forgotten mechanics liens** that kill deals or require expensive quiet title actions.

The strategic advantage of "clean paper" in owner financing cannot be overstated. Unlike bank loans with automated underwriting and standardized terms, seller carry-back notes can be crafted with **subordination clauses** allowing future institutional debt, **substitution of collateral** clauses that unlock exit flexibility, and **principal-only periods** that conserve cash during value-add years. The cost: drafting precision and seller relationship management.

This report provides the legal templates, clause language, and decision frameworks necessary for an investor to act as their own transaction manager through initial diligence, armed to spot deal-killing title defects, redline PSAs with protective contingencies, and structure owner financing that serves strategic flexibility rather than lender whim.

## The Anatomy of the 5-50 Unit Purchase & Sale Agreement

### Why Standard PSA Forms Fail Vintage Multifamily Assets

Most standard commercial PSAs (NCAR, REBAC, or lender-provided templates) assume institutional-grade properties: recent construction, professional tenancy, clean title, and reliable insurance markets. These templates are deficient for pre-1990s multifamily because they:

- **Do not address insurance bindability contingencies** (hard market means coverage might be denied or cost 3-4x normal premiums)
- **Lack specific inspection deadlines for hidden defect discovery** (aluminum wiring, polybutylene, deferred electrical upgrades)
- **Provide weak representations** from sellers on tenant lease status, rent roll accuracy, and verbal concessions
- **Fail to protect the buyer** if disclosure of unpermitted units (ADUs, extra bedrooms) emerges post-offer
- **Omit escrow holdbacks** for identified but minor repairs that seller may never cure

### Critical Clause Checklist for Vintage Assets

#### 1. Insurance Contingency Language

Standard inspection contingency does not protect against insurance market rejection. Use:

> **Insurance Bindability Contingency:** Buyer shall obtain binding (not preliminary) quotes for commercial general liability, property, and workers' compensation coverage within **[14 days]** of Effective Date. If insurance carriers require material repairs (e.g., roof replacement, aluminum wiring remediation, sprinkler/alarm upgrades) as a condition of coverage, or if annual insurance costs exceed **[X% of projected NOI]**, Buyer may terminate this Agreement and receive earnest money refund. Seller shall cooperate by providing any prior loss history, claims summaries, and property condition reports to Buyer's insurance broker. If cost/bindability issues emerge, Buyer and Seller shall negotiate in good faith for **[7 days]** before Buyer exercises termination right.

**Why this matters:** In 2025-2026, multifamily insurers are tightening underwriting on pre-1980s properties. If you discover aluminum wiring or flat tar-paper roofs, carriers may impose 5% wind deductibles, exclude certain perils, or demand full roof replacement before binding. A standard inspection contingency does not trigger if the building passes visual inspection—it fails because insurance won't cover it. This clause protects you.

#### 2. Sewer Scope & Electrical Inspection Contingency

> **Specialized Inspections:** Buyer shall conduct sewer scoping and electrical panel inspection within **[21 days]** of Effective Date. Sewer scoping shall include camera inspection to identify root intrusion, offset joints, or breaks; electrical inspection shall verify compliance with current code for aluminum wiring, improper connections, and missing grounds. If sewer repairs exceed **$[X]** or electrical work exceeds **$[X]**, Buyer may either (a) terminate this Agreement with earnest money refund, or (b) require Seller to escrow **$[Repair Amount]** at closing to be released upon completion of repairs verified by licensed contractor and Buyer's inspector. Repairs not completed within **[90 days]** of closing shall vest the escrowed funds to Buyer.

**Timeline note:** Commercial inspection periods are typically 21-30 days; residential 7-14 days. For vintage multifamily, push for 21+ days given the need for sewer scoping (specialized contractor) and electrical review (may require licensed electrician if aluminum wiring is suspected).

#### 3. "Subject to Repairs" Insurance Binder Language

When seller has deferred maintenance, you may need a temporary insurance binder that excludes the defective system:

> **Temporary Insurance Binder:** Seller shall obtain (or Buyer shall obtain and charge to Seller via holdback) a temporary property insurance binder effective at closing that covers the property for fire/extended coverage only, excluding **[specify systems: electrical, roof, plumbing, HVAC]** until Buyer certifies completion of repairs by licensed contractor. Once repairs are complete and verified, Buyer shall obtain standard commercial coverage and the excluded items shall be added back to the policy. Any cost differential between temporary and standard policies shall be borne by Seller via escrow reduction.

**Operational reality:** Your lender will require full coverage at closing. If the property has bad wiring, most carriers won't bind without remediation. This clause ensures you close on time while protecting the seller from financing your repairs permanently.

#### 4. Rent Roll Verification & Estoppel Contingency

> **Tenant Lease Verification:** Within **[14 days]** of Effective Date, Seller shall deliver to Buyer a **Certified Rent Roll** identifying all tenants, lease commencement/expiration dates, monthly rent, security deposits, pet fees, and any lease amendments or verbal concessions. Buyer shall obtain **Tenant Estoppel Certificates** from **[100%]** of tenants within **[21 days]** of Effective Date. If estoppels reveal (a) rent discrepancies exceeding **[2%]** of annual NOI, (b) unauthorized occupants or subtenants, (c) unpaid rent exceeding **[one month]**, or (d) material lease defaults not disclosed by Seller, Buyer may (i) terminate this Agreement with earnest money refund, or (ii) require Seller to indemnify Buyer for the difference (reduced from purchase price or held in escrow).

**Why this is critical:** Mom-and-pop sellers often operate on verbal agreements, miss rent from tenants they like, and never formalize concessions. When you pull estoppels, you'll discover half the rent roll doesn't match the seller's "financials." This clause forces the seller to either reconcile or face deal termination.

#### 5. Undisclosed Deferred Maintenance / "Defect Adjustment"

> **Undisclosed Defects:** If, during Buyer's due diligence, Buyer discovers material deferred maintenance not disclosed by Seller in the Property Condition Disclosure (e.g., foundation cracks exceeding 1/4", missing roofing sections, failed HVAC, water intrusion, deteriorated plumbing), and if the estimated repair cost exceeds **$[X]**, Buyer may either (a) terminate with earnest money refund, or (b) require Seller to fund an escrow in the amount of **[110% of Repair Estimate]** to be held at closing and released to Buyer upon completion of repairs verified by licensed contractor. If Seller fails to fund escrow or disputes repair estimate, either party may hire a mutually-agreeable third-party engineer/inspector to resolve, cost split 50/50.

**Advantage:** This clause prevents the "seller walkaway" when you discover bad news mid-diligence. It forces them to either fund repairs in escrow or let you terminate. Many sellers, faced with a funded escrow obligation, will renegotiate the price instead—and you keep the earnest money.

### Redlining Guide: What to Strike from Seller's Standard Contract

When a seller or their agent provides their "standard" PSA (often a residential form misapplied to commercial), redline aggressively:

1. **"As-Is" Clauses Without Contingency Rights:** Strike or amend to allow termination if undisclosed defects emerge. Pure "as-is" sales require full diligence before offer; in a hard market, you can't do that fast enough.

2. **Single "Inspection Contingency" Without Specificity:** Replace with itemized contingencies for sewer, electrical, and insurance. A generic "subject to inspection" is too vague and judges will interpret narrowly.

3. **Non-Refundable Earnest Money:** Never agree to this for properties with unknown conditions. Standard is refundable until diligence period ends, then non-refundable unless Seller breaches.

4. **Seller Financing "Personal Note" (No Deed of Trust):** If seller-carry is contemplated, always require a Deed of Trust securing the note. Without it, you have unsecured debt and no right to foreclose if the buyer defaults. This will be addressed in the owner financing section.

5. **Vague Default Language in Owner Financing:** Strike any language that says "reasonable default" or "as determined by Seller." Replace with specific trigger: "Default occurs if payment is not received within 10 days of due date, or if property is transferred without consent, or if property taxes remain unpaid for 60+ days."

6. **No Subordination Clause (If You Plan Future Debt):** If seller is financing the deal and you anticipate construction debt for value-add, your PSA must require the seller note to subordinate to future institutional debt. Without this, no lender will close on your renovation loan.

## Mastering Owner Financing & The "Creative" Stack

### The Note vs. The Deed of Trust: Clarifying the Distinction

**The Promissory Note** is the borrower's written *promise to pay* the seller. It specifies:
- Principal amount
- Interest rate (fixed or variable)
- Payment frequency (monthly, quarterly, annual)
- Maturity date (when the note is fully due)
- Prepayment penalties (if any)
- Late fees
- Default triggers

The note is **not recorded** and does not, by itself, give the seller a right to foreclose if the buyer defaults. It is merely contractual evidence of debt.

**The Deed of Trust** (or Mortgage in some states) is the *security instrument* that gives the seller a lien on the property. It specifies:
- The trustee (a neutral third party who holds the lien)
- The beneficiary (the seller as note holder)
- The trustor (the buyer)
- The property description and legal description
- Events of default and remedies (foreclosure rights)
- The relationship between the note and the deed of trust

The deed of trust **is recorded** with the county recorder and creates a public lien. If the buyer defaults, the deed of trust allows the seller (or the trustee on the seller's behalf) to initiate foreclosure and sell the property to satisfy the debt.

**Example:** You buy a 10-unit building for $1.2M with $300K down and a seller note of $900K. The seller gives you a Promissory Note (the promise to pay $900K at 7% over 10 years, monthly payments of ~$10.5K). You also execute a Deed of Trust naming a title company as trustee, the seller as beneficiary, and your property as collateral. If you miss 3 months of payments, the seller can instruct the trustee to begin foreclosure, eventually resulting in a trustee's sale where the property is sold to recover the $900K debt.

### The Promissory Note: Critical Clauses for Flexibility

#### 1. Subordination Clause (For Construction Debt)

When you acquire a property with seller financing and plan to refinance or secure construction debt, the seller's note must subordinate to the new lender's position. Otherwise, no bank will lend you money because the seller's lien takes priority.

**Sample Language:**

> **Subordination:** The Seller's beneficial interest in the Deed of Trust shall be subordinate and junior to any construction loan, permanent refinancing, or institutional debt secured by the Property, provided that:
> 
> (a) The principal balance of any senior debt does not exceed **$[X]** (typically 80% LTV);
> 
> (b) Buyer provides Seller with written notice of the senior debt terms at least **[10 days]** before closing;
> 
> (c) Seller has the right to pay off this Note in full if the senior lender requires it;
> 
> (d) Any proceeds from refinancing or sale shall be applied first to senior debt, then to this Note, pro-rata if insufficient funds.

**Negotiation tactic:** Unsophisticated sellers resist subordination because they fear losing their security. Reassure them: subordination only applies *if* you get institutional financing (which takes longer and has stricter underwriting). You're protecting your ability to refinance and improve the property, which increases its value and makes their note more likely to be repaid.

#### 2. Substitution of Collateral Clause

This is one of the most underutilized clauses in owner financing. It allows you to *move* the lien from one property to another (e.g., if you sell the building and buy a different one, the seller's lien moves with you). This is valuable for multifamily operators building portfolios.

**Sample Language:**

> **Substitution of Collateral:** The Maker may, at its sole discretion and without Holder's prior approval, substitute other real property as collateral for this Note, provided that:
> 
> (a) The substitute collateral is equal to or greater in value than the current collateral (evidenced by appraisal at Maker's expense);
> 
> (b) The substitute collateral generates equal or greater net operating income than the current collateral;
> 
> (c) Maker provides Holder with written notice of the substitution and a new Deed of Trust secured by the substitute collateral, executed and recorded;
> 
> (d) The substitute collateral is free of liens other than those expressly permitted (e.g., property taxes, utility easements);
> 
> (e) Maker shall pay all costs of substitution (appraisal, recording, title insurance update).

**Leverage:** You tell the seller, "I may need to move this loan to another property in 3-5 years if I sell this building or consolidate my portfolio. This clause lets me do that without coming back to you for permission." Most sellers agree because it benefits them too: if you hit a bad market and want to walk away from Property A, substitution lets you give them Property B instead—recovering the debt without foreclosure.

#### 3. Principal-Only Period (Interest-Only Payments for Value-Add)

In a value-add scenario, you may want to pay **interest-only** for the first 2-3 years while you're capturing deferred maintenance and stabilizing the asset. This improves early cash flow.

**Sample Language:**

> **Interest-Only Period:** 
> 
> For the first **[36 months]** after the Closing Date (the "Interest-Only Period"), Maker shall pay **interest only** on the outstanding principal balance, calculated at the stated annual interest rate of **[7.0%]**, divided by 12, and payable monthly on the **[1st]** day of each month, commencing **[Date]**.
> 
> Principal payments shall commence on **[Date, typically Month 37]** and shall be amortized over the remaining **[10 - 3 = 7 years]** in equal monthly installments.
> 
> If, at the end of the Interest-Only Period, Maker has not commenced principal and interest payments, or if Maker requests extension of the Interest-Only Period, Holder's written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) is required.

**Cash flow example:** You acquire a 20-unit, $2M building with $500K down and a $1.5M seller note at 7% over 10 years. 
- Standard amortization: ~$17.6K/month
- Interest-only for 36 months: ~$8.75K/month
- Savings: ~$8.85K/month × 36 months = ~$318K in the first 3 years

You use that cash to upgrade units, evict problem tenants, and raise rents. By Year 4, stabilized NOI supports the full $17.6K payment.

#### 4. Prepayment Penalty Language (Or Waiver of It)

Many seller notes include prepayment penalties to protect the seller if the buyer refinances early. For owner financing, consider either:

**Option A (Seller-Friendly):**
> **Prepayment Penalty:** If Maker prepays any principal balance (through refinancing, sale, or voluntary payment) within **[5 years]** of Closing, Maker shall pay a prepayment penalty of **[3.0%]** of the prepaid principal amount. After **[5 years]**, no penalty applies. This penalty does not apply to prepayment of interest-only amounts or if Maker prepays within **[60 days]** of the Interest-Only Period ending.

**Option B (Buyer-Friendly, For Negotiation):**
> **No Prepayment Penalty:** Maker may prepay any or all of the principal balance at any time without penalty. Holder acknowledges that Maker may refinance or sell the property and desires flexibility to do so.

**Strategy:** If you're offering the seller a competitive interest rate (e.g., 7% when institutional 5-year fixed is 6.5%), the seller may accept no prepayment penalty in exchange for the rate premium. If rates are low and the seller's rate is attractive (e.g., 5% when institutional is 6%), the seller will want a prepayment penalty to protect against refinancing loss.

### The Deed of Trust: Structuring for Clarity and Enforceability

The Deed of Trust must clearly specify default events and remedy procedures. Vague language leads to disputes and, in foreclosure, can be challenged by the borrower.

#### 1. Default Triggers (Specific & Objective)

> **Events of Default:** The occurrence of any one or more of the following shall constitute an "Event of Default":
> 
> (a) **Non-Payment of Principal or Interest:** If any installment of principal or interest due under the Note remains unpaid for **[10 days]** after the due date;
> 
> (b) **Property Tax Default:** If Maker fails to pay property taxes or HOA assessments within **[30 days]** of delinquency;
> 
> (c) **Insurance Lapse:** If Maker fails to maintain required property insurance (naming Holder as loss payee) or if insurance is canceled or materially reduced;
> 
> (d) **Unauthorized Transfer:** If Maker conveys, encumbers, or transfers any interest in the Property without Holder's written consent;
> 
> (e) **Breach of Covenants:** If Maker breaches any material covenant in this Deed of Trust or the Note and fails to cure within **[30 days]** of written notice by Holder;
> 
> (f) **Material Deterioration:** If the Property is abandoned, substantially damaged, or materially deteriorates due to Maker's negligence or failure to maintain.

**Enforceability point:** Courts scrutinize "mysterious" defaults. Being specific (e.g., "10 days late") avoids challenges. Avoid language like "material breach" without definition.

#### 2. Notice & Cure Rights

> **Notice of Default:** If an Event of Default occurs, Holder shall provide Maker with written notice specifying the default and, where applicable, the required cure. Maker shall have **[10 days]** (for payment defaults) or **[30 days]** (for non-payment defaults) to cure the default. If Maker cures within the notice period, the default is waived and does not constitute a default for subsequent notice purposes.

**Legal protection:** In judicial foreclosure states, providing notice and cure rights strengthens your position and protects against claims of unjust enrichment or unconscionable enforcement.

#### 3. Foreclosure Procedure (Judicial vs. Non-Judicial)

The Deed of Trust should specify the state law governing foreclosure. This is critical because foreclosure timelines and procedures vary dramatically by state.

**For Non-Judicial Foreclosure States (CA, TX, AZ, NV, etc.):**

> **Non-Judicial Foreclosure:** Upon Event of Default, Holder may instruct the Trustee to sell the Property at public auction without court involvement. The Trustee shall (a) record a Notice of Default with the county recorder, (b) provide written notice to Maker at the address stated in this Deed of Trust, (c) wait **[state-required period, typically 90-120 days]**, (d) publish notice of sale in a newspaper of general circulation, and (e) conduct a trustee's sale at the county courthouse steps or other designated location. The Property shall be sold to the highest bidder. Proceeds shall be applied: first, to trustee fees and costs; second, to Holder's debt (principal, interest, and attorney fees); third, to junior lienholders in order of lien; and fourth, to Maker if any surplus remains.

**Timeline advantage:** Non-judicial foreclosure in CA/TX is 120-150 days start to finish. Judicial foreclosure in NY/NJ is 1-2 years due to court schedules.

**For Judicial Foreclosure States (NY, NJ, PA, IL, etc.):**

> **Judicial Foreclosure:** Upon Event of Default, Holder shall file a foreclosure action in the District/Circuit Court for the county in which the Property is located. Maker shall be served with the complaint and notice of default. Maker may respond and defend within **[20-30 days]** (per state law). If Maker fails to respond or Holder prevails, the court shall enter a judgment of foreclosure. A court-appointed commissioner shall then schedule and conduct a judicial sale of the Property at public auction. Proceeds shall be applied per state law priority: Holder's debt, junior liens, and any surplus to Maker.

**Timeline reality:** Plan for 12-24 months. Judicial foreclosure provides due process but is slow. In distressed markets, borrowers may delay foreclosure through bankruptcy or disputes over notice.

#### 4. Acceleration Clause

> **Acceleration:** Upon Event of Default, Holder may declare the entire unpaid balance of the Note, plus accrued interest, immediately due and payable. Maker shall have **[10 days]** from the date of acceleration notice to pay the full amount; otherwise, Holder may initiate foreclosure. Acceleration is automatic upon non-payment of principal for **[90 days]** but requires written notice for other defaults.

**Leverage:** Acceleration allows you to either get paid in full immediately or begin foreclosure. Without acceleration, you'd be limited to recovering one month's payment at a time, which is inefficient.

### Advanced Structuring: The Wraparound Mortgage

A wraparound is a specialized form of owner financing where the buyer does not directly assume the seller's existing mortgage. Instead:

- The seller keeps the existing mortgage in their name
- The buyer executes a note and deed of trust to the seller for the "wrapped" amount
- The buyer makes payments to the seller, who forwards payments to the original lender
- The buyer benefits from the original (lower) mortgage rate spread

**Example:** 
- Seller owes $600K on a 3.5% loan (grandfathered low rate)
- Buyer pays $100K down, $900K wrapped
- Buyer pays seller 6.5% on the $900K wrap
- Seller collects 6.5% from buyer, pays 3.5% on the $600K original loan, and pockets the 3% spread
- Seller's equity is protected by the original mortgage being in their name

#### The Due-On-Sale Risk

The critical legal risk in wraparounds is the **due-on-sale clause**. Most mortgages contain language that allows the original lender to demand full repayment if the property is sold or transferred (which a wraparound technically is, even though possession doesn't change).

**Texas Real Estate Code § 5.016 addresses this:**
> "A seller must disclose to the buyer, in writing and before the buyer is bound by any executory contract... the existence of any lien or claim against the property, including a mortgage, deed of trust, or other security instrument that will not be discharged or released by the consummation of the sale."

The disclosure must explain the risk: if the original lender exercises the due-on-sale clause, the seller is liable for the entire mortgage and could face foreclosure.

**Mitigating the Due-On-Sale Risk:**

1. **Silent Assumption (Risky):** Simply make payments to the seller without formally notifying the original lender. This works if the property performs well and the original loan is "seasoned" (old). Lenders rarely enforce due-on-sale in performing accounts. **Downside:** If the borrower defaults or the property is sold, the lender discovers the transfer and can accelerate.

2. **Seller Stays as Owner of Record:** In a wraparound, the seller remains the official owner (deed is in seller's name). The buyer receives equitable title (beneficial ownership). The original lender sees no change of ownership on county records and may not know about the wrap. **Downside:** Seller remains liable for property taxes, insurance, and the original mortgage.

3. **Judicial Enforcement is Rare:** Courts and legal practice recognize that lenders rarely enforce due-on-sale clauses in performing loans, especially if the property is well-maintained and payments are current. The Garn-St. Germain Act (federal law) allows lenders to exercise due-on-sale, but state laws and lender practice vary. **Downside:** "Rare" is not "never." If markets deteriorate and the property falls in value, the lender may use due-on-sale as an exit.

4. **Lender Approval (Best):** In a strong market, ask the original lender to approve the wraparound or provide a subordination agreement. Some institutional lenders, facing a market where rates are rising and quality borrowers are scarce, may approve rather than foreclose. **Downside:** Lender approval requires disclosure and may trigger prepayment or refinancing requirements.

**Practical Structure for Wraparounds in Texas (and Non-Judicial States):**

For a $2M acquisition with $500K seller-carry wrap:
- Original mortgage: $1.2M at 3.5% (assume 20 years remaining)
- Wrap note: $500K at 6.5%, amortized 10 years
- Buyer puts $300K down, pays cash to close
- Buyer makes monthly payments to seller of ~$5,300/month on the wrap
- Seller forwards ~$6,500/month to original lender and keeps the difference

**Deed of Trust Language for Wraparound:**

> **Wraparound Deed of Trust:** This Deed of Trust secures a note in the principal amount of **$500,000** (the "Wrapped Amount"), which represents Maker's obligation to reimburse Holder for Maker's assumption of Holder's responsibilities with respect to the underlying mortgage encumbering the Property. The Property is currently encumbered by a mortgage held by **[Original Lender Name]**, in the principal amount of approximately **$1,200,000**, bearing interest at **3.5% per annum**, with a remaining term of approximately **[20 years]**. Maker acknowledges that Holder retains the obligation to pay the underlying mortgage and that Maker's payments to Holder are the source of funds for Holder to service the underlying mortgage.
> 
> Holder shall maintain the underlying mortgage in good standing. Maker shall reimburse Holder for all payments made by Holder to the underlying lender. If the underlying mortgage is accelerated due to a due-on-sale clause or other trigger, Holder shall immediately notify Maker, and Maker shall have **[30 days]** to either (a) pay the entire underlying mortgage to satisfy the acceleration, or (b) refinance the underlying mortgage in Maker's name. If Maker fails to do either, Holder may initiate foreclosure on this Deed of Trust.

#### Servicing Arrangements & Escrow

In a wraparound, a neutral third party (typically a title company or servicing company) often collects the buyer's payment, forwards it to the original lender, and remits the seller's portion. This protects both parties and ensures transparency.

**Escrow/Servicing Clause:**

> **Loan Servicing:** The parties may elect to use a third-party loan servicer to collect payments from Maker, disburse to the underlying lender, and remit the net amount to Holder. The servicer shall be selected by mutual agreement and shall charge a servicing fee of **[0.5% of principal annually]**, split between Maker and Holder. All parties authorize the servicer to collect, hold in escrow, and disburse funds in accordance with this Deed of Trust.

**Advantage:** If the buyer defaults, the servicer's records prove the seller was making payments on the underlying mortgage and were not at fault for the due-on-sale trigger.

## Title Review & Due Diligence Defense

### Understanding the Title Commitment: Schedule A, B-1, and B-2

A Title Commitment is the title company's preliminary report of what they will insure. It consists of three parts:

**Schedule A (Current Owner & Coverage):**
- Names of the current owner(s)
- Legal description of the property
- Type of title policy (ALTA or standard)
- Amount of insurance
- Effective date of the commitment

**Schedule B-1 (Requirements to Issue Insurance):**
- All requirements that **must be satisfied before closing** for the title company to issue the final title policy
- Examples: payoff of existing mortgages, recording of new deed, proof of authority (if seller is a trust or LLC), tax certifications

**Schedule B-2 (Exceptions to Coverage):**
- All matters that **will NOT be covered** by the title insurance policy
- Examples: utility easements, setback restrictions, HOA covenants, survey exceptions

### Schedule B-2 Deep Dive: Common Vintage Property Issues

#### 1. Utility Easements (Standard Exception)

Utility companies have rights to access your property for power lines, water mains, gas lines, etc.

> Schedule B-2 Exception: "Right of [Company Name] to maintain, repair, rebuild, and operate and use for public utility purposes an underground gas line located across the property."

**Impact:** Minor. You can build over utility easements in most cases, but the utility company can excavate if maintenance is needed. Not a deal-killer, but it affects site planning.

**How to address:** Verify the easement alignment and depth with the utility company before approving plans for major structures.

#### 2. Restrictions, Covenants & Conditions (CC&Rs)

Older properties often have deed restrictions limiting use.

> Schedule B-2 Exception: "Restrictions, Covenants, and Conditions as set forth in Deed recorded in Book 1234, Page 567, including but not limited to restriction on use to residential purposes only and prohibition on commercial advertising."

**Impact:** Can be severe. If the deed restriction prohibits multifamily rental or requires deed approval for changes, it limits your value-add strategy.

**How to address:** 
- Obtain and read the entire original restrictive covenant document
- Determine if the restriction is still enforceable (many expire by their terms or if surrounding properties have violated them)
- If enforceable and problematic, file a "Petition to Modify or Remove Restrictive Covenant" or obtain a quiet title action
- Cost: $5K-$20K for a quiet title action; timeline: 3-6 months

#### 3. Unrecorded or Long-Forgotten Easements

Older properties may have easements that were recorded decades ago and are not actively used.

> Schedule B-2 Exception: "Easement for driveway purposes granted to neighboring property owner, recorded in Book 456, Page 789 (1987)."

**Impact:** Moderate. If the easement permits the neighbor to cross your property, it may limit parking or use of certain areas.

**How to address:**
- Verify current use: visit the neighbor's property and confirm if they actually use the easement
- If unused for **20+ years** (varies by state), you may file a "Notice of Abandonment" or seek quiet title action
- If actively used, negotiate with the neighbor to relocate the easement or acknowledge it in writing

#### 4. Mechanics Liens (Clouds on Title)

Old unpaid contractor liens can appear on title, even if they're from prior owners.

> Schedule B-2 Exception: "Mechanics lien filed by [Contractor Name] on [Date], recorded in Book 2100, Page 345, in the amount of $15,000."

**Impact:** High. A mechanics lien clouds title and prevents you from selling or refinancing until it's released.

**How to address:**
- Determine if the lien is against the property or the prior owner
- If the contractor still has rights to foreclose, negotiate a release (offer 50% of the lien amount to the contractor for a quitclaim)
- If the lien is stale (**typically 6 months to 5 years depending on state**), file a "Notice of Expiration" or "Petition to Remove Mechanics Lien"
- Verify the lien was properly served and noted within the state's statutory deadline
- If the lien is older than the deadline (without renewal), petition the court to remove it as a cloud on title

**Texas Example:** A mechanics lien must be foreclosed within **2 years** of the date of the lien. If filed in 2020, it expires in 2022. If you're in 2025 and the lien is still on the title, you can file a "Petition to Remove Mechanics Lien as Expired" and have it removed.

#### 5. Oil & Gas Reservations

Older rural or suburban properties may have oil/gas rights reserved to a previous owner.

> Schedule B-2 Exception: "Oil and Gas Reservation to [Original Grantor], dated [1950], reserved as to all hydrocarbons under the property."

**Impact:** Low to high depending on location. If you're in a productive oil/gas region (TX, OK, CA, CO), the reservation holder may have development rights. In non-productive areas, it's essentially a cloud that rarely affects operations.

**How to address:**
- Search for the current holder of the oil/gas reservation (heirs, assignees)
- Obtain a subordination agreement or quitclaim from them, typically for a small negotiation fee or acknowledgment that no development is planned
- If the original grantor is deceased and heirs unknown, consider title insurance endorsement to delete the exception (title company may issue a policy exception to "Oil and Gas Reservations and Developments")

#### 6. Survey Exceptions ("Survey Matters")

Most title policies carve out survey-related matters (encroachments, boundary line disputes, etc.).

> Schedule B-2 Exception: "Survey exception, as to boundary lines and encroachments."

**Impact:** Moderate. Without a survey, you don't know if your building encroaches on the neighbor's property or vice versa.

**How to address:**
- Obtain a current ALTA survey from a licensed surveyor ($500-$2,000 depending on property size)
- If the survey reveals encroachments, determine if they're minor (fence on the line) or major (building overhang onto neighbor's land)
- Negotiate a "boundary line agreement" with the neighbor or obtain title insurance coverage for encroachments

### Red Flags Specific to Vintage Multifamily

| Red Flag | Severity | Action |
|----------|----------|--------|
| Mechanics lien from unknown contractor (>2 years old) | HIGH | Petition to remove; require seller to pay off if current |
| Multiple transfers within 5 years to different LLCs | MEDIUM | Investigate ownership chain; verify no HUD/foreclosure sales |
| Deed restrictions limiting to "single-family residential" | HIGH | Quiet title action required; 3-6 months, $10K+ cost |
| Easement to neighboring parcel (active use) | MEDIUM | Negotiate relocation; clarify impact on site plan |
| Missing parcel in legal description (historical title split) | HIGH | Boundary line agreement or quiet title action |
| Oil/gas reservation to unknown heir | MEDIUM | Search heirs; obtain quitclaim or subordination |
| Utility easement across building footprint | MEDIUM | Verify depth/alignment with utility; may need relocation |
| HOA lien for unpaid assessments (prior owner) | HIGH | Require seller to pay from escrow; verify past years current |

### The "Go / No-Go" Title Decision Matrix

When you pull the title commitment, ask yourself:

**GREEN LIGHT (Standard Issues, Acceptable):**
- Utility easements not affecting building
- Survey exceptions (obtain survey to confirm they're harmless)
- CC&Rs that don't prohibit multifamily use
- Oil/gas reservations in non-productive areas

**YELLOW LIGHT (Resolvable with Time/Cost):**
- Mechanics liens older than foreclosure deadline (can petition removal)
- Unrecorded easements (can obtain quitclaim from neighbor or subordination)
- Old deed restrictions (can quiet title if no longer enforced)
- Missing parcel (can resolve with boundary line agreement or merger)

**RED LIGHT (Deal-Killer or Requires Major Negotiation):**
- Deed restriction prohibiting multifamily use (requires quiet title action; 3-6 months)
- Active mechanics lien from unpaid contractor (must be paid to clear)
- HOA lien for unpaid assessments (must be paid or indemnified)
- Title defect preventing insurance (e.g., missing heirs, conflicting ownership)
- Encroachment of building onto neighbor's property without agreement

**Process:**
1. Receipt of title commitment → 5 business days to review
2. If RED issues, notify seller: "We need you to resolve X by [date] or we'll terminate"
3. If YELLOW issues, request title company work or negotiate escrow holdback
4. If GREEN issues, proceed to closing unless title insurance quotes new exceptions

## The Estoppel & Lease Audit: Truth Serum for NOI

### Why Estoppels Are Critical (And Why Sellers Fear Them)

An estoppel certificate is a sworn statement by each tenant confirming:
- Lease commencement and expiration dates
- Current monthly rent
- Any rent paid in advance or owed in arrears
- Lease concessions or amendments
- Whether the landlord has performed required improvements
- Whether the landlord is in default

The purpose is to "estop" (prevent) the tenant from later claiming that lease terms are different or that the landlord owes them money.

**Why sellers fear estoppels:** Mom-and-pop operators often:
- Make verbal promises to tenants (e.g., "your rent stays at $1,000 for another year")
- Forgive rent arrears without documenting it
- Allow subtenants or occupants not on the lease
- Fail to make promised repairs

When tenants sign an estoppel, they're committing to whatever is on that document. If the seller promised "$1,000 rent forever" but the estoppel says "$1,200," the tenant's estoppel contradicts the oral promise. This creates liability for the seller.

### Handling Estoppel Discrepancies

When an estoppel contradicts the seller's rent roll or T12 financials, **do not close** without resolution. Common scenarios:

#### Scenario 1: Estoppel Shows Unpaid Rent Owed

Seller's rent roll says tenant owes $0. Estoppel says tenant owes $2,000.

**Action:**
- Request proof from tenant: cancelled checks or payment receipts showing the $2,000 was paid
- If tenant can't prove payment, require seller to escrow $2,000 at closing to be held until tenant pays or Buyer can evict
- Alternatively, require seller to execute a subordination acknowledging the tenant may offset the owed rent against future payments

#### Scenario 2: Estoppel Reveals Oral Lease Amendment Not on Lease Document

Tenant says, "The landlord promised to fix the roof before my lease expires and give me $200/month rent credit until it's done." Lease document says no such thing.

**Action:**
- Require seller to either (a) execute a written amendment to the lease formalizing the $200 credit, or (b) escrow enough funds to cover the roof repair and tenant's rent credits
- Do not pass this obligation to the buyer

#### Scenario 3: Estoppel Reveals Unauthorized Subtenant

Seller's lease is with "John Doe." Estoppel reveals John's daughter "Jane Doe" and her boyfriend live there, and Jane pays John $500/month to cover part of rent.

**Action:**
- This is a lease violation (unauthorized occupancy/subletting)
- Require seller to either evict Jane/boyfriend or execute a formal sublease amendment
- If Buyer intends to keep Jane as a sub-tenant, get a formal sublease with Jane and John, subordinate to the master lease, specifying her liability if John defaults

#### Scenario 4: Rent Roll Inflation (Estoppel Shows Lower Rent)

Seller's rent roll says Unit 5 = $1,500/month. Estoppel says $1,200/month.

**Action:**
- Request the actual lease from the seller; compare to estoppel
- If lease says $1,200 and seller just inflated the rent roll, reduce purchase price by the present value of the difference
- Example: $300/month × 12 months × Cap Rate (5%) = $72,000 purchase price reduction

### Identifying Protected Tenant Classes

In jurisdictions with strong tenant protections (CA, NY, NJ, Boston, DC), certain tenants cannot be evicted or have rent raises limited. This directly affects your value-add NOI capture.

#### California AB 1482 (Statewide Rent Control)

Applies to **all multifamily buildings 15+ years old** unless exempt.

**Cap on rent increases:** 5% + regional CPI, not to exceed 10%, per year.

**Exemptions:**
- Buildings built after 2003 (for first 30 years)
- Single-family homes (unless rented by corporation)
- Owner-occupied duplexes (if owner in one unit before tenant moved in)

**Just Cause Eviction:** Landlord must provide "good cause" to evict, including:
- Non-payment of rent
- Violation of lease terms
- Nuisance/criminal activity
- Refusing to sign new lease with "reasonable" terms
- Landlord intent to occupy unit for personal use (with exceptions for seniors/disabled)
- Property demolition or withdrawal from rental market

**Value-add impact:**
- If you acquire a 30-unit building with 20 units at $1,200/month and market rent is $1,500, you can only raise $20 × $300 = $6,000/month max over multiple years
- At 5% annual cap, it takes 5 years to reach market rent
- Loss-to-lease capture is severely limited

**Lease audit strategy:** Identify all tenants and their current rents. Calculate what rent increases are possible under AB 1482. Model the value-add capture over 5-year hold.

#### New York Good Cause Eviction Law (Effective April 2024)

Applies to **most multifamily units built before 2009**, with exemptions for high-rent units and small landlords.

**Rent increase caps:** 
- Annual increases capped at the lesser of:
  - 5% + annual inflation (CPI), or
  - 10%
  - Different percentages for different geographies (NYC, upstate, etc.)

**"Good Cause" for eviction includes:**
- Non-payment of "reasonable" rent (defined as FMR + 10%)
- Lease violation or unlawful activity
- Refusal to sign lease renewal with "reasonable" changes
- Owner intent to occupy, demolish, or withdraw (with exceptions)

**High-rent exemption:** Units renting for more than 245% of Fair Market Rent (FMR) are exempt. (Example: In NYC, a 1BR with FMR $3,000 would be exempt if renting above $7,350.)

**Small landlord exemption:** Owners of ≤10 units in NY State are exempt.

**Value-add impact:**
- If your 20-unit building has one high-rent unit (penthouse), only that unit is exempt
- All others are subject to good cause and rent caps
- Model conservatively assuming 5% annual increases post-acquisition

**Lease audit strategy:** Identify tenant rent relative to local FMR. Determine exemptions. Calculate rent growth constraints. You may find that your 25% value-add NOI capture is reduced to 12% after accounting for rent control.

### Just Cause Eviction Procedures & Notice Periods

**California Example (AB 1482):**
- Landlord must provide 30-day notice to vacate for month-to-month tenancies or lease expiration
- If evicting for "at-fault" cause (non-payment, breach, nuisance), landlord must provide 3-day notice to cure or quit
- If tenant doesn't cure within 3 days, landlord can file unlawful detainer (eviction lawsuit)
- Court process typically takes 30-60 days if uncontested
- **Timeline: 3 days notice + 30-60 days court = 33-63 days minimum to actual eviction**

**New York Example (Good Cause Law):**
- Landlord must provide notice of good cause if non-renewing (timing varies)
- If tenant challenges non-renewal, landlord must prove good cause in housing court
- Tenant has 90 days to respond; court decides
- If evicting for non-payment, landlord provides 10-day notice; tenant can pay to stop eviction
- Court process is more formal; judges scrutinize evidence
- **Timeline: 10+ days notice + 60-180 days court = 70-190 days minimum**

**Texas Example (No Just Cause):**
- Landlord can evict for non-payment (3-day notice) or lease violation (3-day notice)
- Tenant has right to cure if notice specifies cure provision
- Unlawful detainer lawsuit filed; judgment typically within 10-30 days if uncontested
- **Timeline: 3 days notice + 10-30 days court = 13-33 days to eviction**

**Underwriting Implications:**

In a value-add scenario where you're evicting non-payers or problem tenants:
- **CA:** Budget 3-4 months per eviction
- **NY:** Budget 4-6 months per eviction
- **TX:** Budget 1-2 months per eviction

This affects your stabilization timeline and cash flow projections.

### Mandatory Disclosures for Vintage Properties

#### Lead Paint Disclosure (Federal)

**Applies to:** All residential property built before 1978.

**Requirement:** Seller must disclose, in writing, any known lead-based paint or lead-hazard information. Disclosure must:
1. Identify the location of known lead paint (if any)
2. Identify the condition of painted surfaces
3. Include a lead warning statement
4. Provide a copy of EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family From Lead In Your Home"
5. Give buyer **10-day inspection period** to test for lead (waivable)

**Penalties for non-disclosure:** Up to $16,000 per property; can be tripled if willful.

**Sample language for vintage PSA:**

> **Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (Federal):** Pursuant to the Real Estate Notification and Disclosure Rule, 40 CFR Part 745, the Property was built in [19XX], prior to the federal ban on lead paint in 1978. Seller represents that it has no knowledge of lead-based paint or lead-hazard conditions on the Property, except as follows: [list any known locations]. Buyer is provided 10 days from Effective Date to conduct a lead inspection by a certified lead inspector. Buyer may waive this inspection right in writing. [Include EPA pamphlet and lead warning statement.]

#### Asbestos Disclosure (State-Specific)

**Applies to:** Some states require disclosure of known asbestos. California, New Jersey, New York, and others mandate inspection or disclosure.

**Typical disclosure:**

> The Property was constructed prior to [year] and may contain asbestos in insulation, floor tiles, pipe wrap, roofing, siding, or other materials. Buyer is advised to have the Property inspected by a certified asbestos inspector if Buyer is concerned about the presence of asbestos.

**Operational reality:** Asbestos in good condition (not deteriorating) poses low risk. If you discover asbestos during renovation, you must hire a licensed abatement contractor. Cost: $5,000-$30,000 depending on extent.

#### Polybutylene Plumbing Disclosure (If Present)

Polybutylene pipes (common in 1980s-1990s) are prone to rupture and often cause insurance coverage denial or premium spikes.

**Disclosure (if discovered):**

> The Property is plumbed with polybutylene piping. Polybutylene pipes are susceptible to deterioration and rupture. Buyer should obtain verification from homeowners insurance carrier that coverage is available for this property. If insurance is declined or limited, Buyer may terminate this Agreement.

**Financial impact:** Many insurance carriers refuse to insure properties with polybutylene without full replacement. Replacement cost: $8,000-$25,000 for a 20-unit building.

#### Aluminum Wiring Disclosure (If Present)

Aluminum branch wiring (common in 1965-1978) creates fire risk at connections.

**Disclosure (if discovered):**

> The Property contains aluminum wiring in [specify: main service, branch circuits, etc.]. Aluminum wiring poses fire risk at connections if improper terminations exist. Buyer should have electrical inspection by licensed electrician and obtain approval from homeowners insurance carrier. If insurance declines coverage or requires remediation, cost estimates shall be provided.

**Remediation options:**
- **Full rewiring:** $15,000-$40,000 for a 20-unit building
- **Copper pigtailing:** Splice aluminum to copper at all outlets/switches; $5,000-$12,000
- **Insurance approval:** Some carriers will approve aluminum if properly installed and connections are clean

### Zoning, Occupancy, and Unpermitted Units

Many pre-1990s multifamily buildings have **unpermitted units**—ADUs, converted storage, or extra bedrooms created without zoning/building permits.

**Exposure:**

1. **Zoning violation:** If zoned for 20 units and you operate 25, city can issue citation and require the 5 units be closed
2. **Insurance denial:** If fire occurs and occupancy exceeds permitted, insurer may deny claims
3. **Financing impact:** Lender will require all units to be permitted or will reduce LTV
4. **Rent roll impact:** If you can't operate unpermitted units, NOI drops

**Investigation:**

- Pull zoning from city/county planning department
- Request certificate of occupancy (C of O) from city
- Cross-reference lease count to permitted unit count
- If discrepancy, request seller provide permits or acknowledge units are unpermitted

**Handling unpermitted units:**

**Option 1 (Conservative):** Eliminate from rent roll; model NOI without unpermitted units. Reduces purchase price.

**Option 2 (Aggressive):** Pursue retroactive permit approval if code compliance can be demonstrated (e.g., unit has its own kitchen, separate entrance, meets square footage). Timing: 2-6 months; cost: $2,000-$5,000.

**Option 3 (Negotiation):** Split the difference—reduce purchase price by the NOI value of unpermitted units; plan for future permit conversion.

## Foreclosure Landscapes: Judicial vs. Non-Judicial

### Understanding the Difference

**Non-Judicial Foreclosure** (Power of Sale):
- Lender (or trustee) can sell the property without court involvement
- Faster: 90-150 days typical
- Less expensive: $2,000-$5,000 in costs
- Common in: CA, TX, AZ, NV, OR, WA, UT, MT, CO
- Process: Notice of Default → Notice of Sale → Trustee's Sale (public auction)

**Judicial Foreclosure** (Court-Ordered):
- Lender must file lawsuit and obtain court judgment
- Slower: 12-24 months typical
- More expensive: $10,000-$50,000+ in attorney fees
- Common in: NY, NJ, PA, IL, MA, CT, DE, FL
- Process: File foreclosure complaint → Serve borrower → Court hearing → Judgment → Judicial sale

### State-by-State Foreclosure Type

**Non-Judicial (Power of Sale) States:**
Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois (some), Indiana (some), Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, West Virginia, Wisconsin, Wyoming

**Judicial States:**
Connecticut, Delaware, District of Columbia, Florida, Illinois (alternative), Indiana (alternative), Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Maine, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Vermont, Wisconsin

**Hybrid (Both Allowed):**
Various states including Arkansas, Maryland, Massachusetts, North Carolina, Tennessee, Utah allow either judicial or non-judicial depending on loan instrument.

### Implications for Borrower in Owner-Financed Deals

#### Scenario 1: You Buy with Seller Financing in a Non-Judicial State (TX)

You default on the seller's note. Seller's rights:

1. Send 3-day notice to cure (you have 3 days to pay or lose property)
2. If you don't pay, seller instructs trustee to issue Notice of Default
3. Trustee publishes notice for 21 days
4. Trustee holds public auction 21 days later
5. Property is sold to highest bidder
6. **You are out in ~45 days**

**Your defense:** Claim the notice of default was improper, or file bankruptcy to trigger automatic stay (delays sale 90+ days).

#### Scenario 2: You Buy with Seller Financing in a Judicial State (NY)

You default. Seller's rights:

1. File foreclosure lawsuit in supreme court
2. You are served; you have 30 days to respond
3. Court schedules hearing (months away)
4. Hearing occurs; judgment entered (if you don't defend or lose)
5. Court orders judicial sale; auctioneer schedules sale
6. Sale occurs, usually 6-12 months after judgment
7. **You are out in 12-24 months**

**Your defense:** Claim defense to foreclosure (e.g., predatory lending, improper notice); court will hear it. Judicial foreclosure provides due process.

### The "Due-on-Sale" Trap in Seller Financing

As discussed in the Wraparound section, the seller's original mortgage may contain a due-on-sale clause. If that clause is triggered:

**In Non-Judicial States:**
- Original lender sends notice of acceleration (loan is due in full)
- Seller must either pay off the original loan immediately or allow it to foreclose
- If seller can't pay, the original lender forecloses on their deed of trust, taking the property
- Seller's note (your debt) becomes unsecured; seller loses collateral
- You (the buyer) are unaffected unless the sale proceeds don't cover both liens

**In Judicial States:**
- Original lender files foreclosure lawsuit
- Process takes 12-24 months
- Both lender's mortgage and seller's note are resolved in the judicial sale
- Proceeds are distributed per lien priority

**Practical impact:** If you're in a non-judicial state and the original mortgage is triggered, you could lose the property in 45-90 days. If judicial, you have more time to refinance or work it out.

## Interconnections: How Legal Frameworks Enable Value-Add Strategy

### Legal Frameworks → Owner Financing (Creative Stack)

The ability to structure owner financing with protective clauses directly enables acquisition in the 5-50 unit space. Here's why:

1. **Subordination + Construction Debt:** Without subordination clauses, you can't layer institutional construction financing on top of seller carry. With them, you can acquire with 30% seller down-carry, 70% construction financing for value-add, and refinance at stabilization.

2. **Substitution of Collateral:** Without this, you're locked into the original property. With it, you can sell the property to another investor, move the seller's lien to a new property in your portfolio, and keep the favorable terms.

3. **Principal-Only Periods:** Without them, early cash flow is consumed by note payments. With them, you use 3 years of interest-only payments to fund unit upgrades and rent growth, then refinance out the seller note.

4. **Customized Default Triggers:** Without specific default language, you're subject to whatever the seller interprets as default. With them, you know exactly what could trigger acceleration and can manage it (e.g., paying property taxes 10 days before deadline to avoid default).

**Example:** You acquire a 30-unit building for $3M using:
- $900K down (30%)
- $1.2M seller carry (40%) at 6.5%, interest-only for 3 years, then 10-year amortization, subordinate to construction debt
- $900K construction financing (30%) for unit upgrades

**Without legal protections:**
- Seller might not subordinate; construction lender won't close
- You can't do the value-add; deal is dead

**With legal protections:**
- Seller subordinates to construction debt per deed of trust language
- Construction lender closes and funds $900K
- You upgrade units, raise rents 20% over 3 years, then refinance
- Refinance payoff: $1.2M seller note + $900K construction debt = $2.1M
- Refi at 70% LTV of $3.6M value = $2.52M; seller note paid off
- You keep $420K cash and own 30% equity

### Legal Compliance → Market Analysis & Rent Rolls (Regulatory Boundaries)

Rent control and just cause eviction laws directly define the boundaries of your rent-growth underwriting. Here's the connection:

**California AB 1482:**
- **Tenant base:** You can't evict 75% of tenants to achieve your rent-growth model
- **Rent growth cap:** You're limited to 5% + CPI per year (10% max)
- **Timeline:** 5-year value-add window = maybe 30-35% cumulative rent growth, not the 50-75% you might achieve in TX/AZ

**Underwriting impact:**
- You model NOI growth assuming 10% annual rent growth for 5 years (1.61x multiplier)
- CA AB 1482 says maximum 7.5% annual (conservative estimate with CPI) = 1.44x multiplier
- Your value-add delta shrinks from $500K to $350K NOI growth
- Cap rate compression (from 5.5% to 5.0%) used to fund your returns is less available

**Lease audit + rent control sync:**
- When you audit leases, you identify each tenant's current rent and protected status
- You model which tenants can be increased (those paying below-market) vs. which are locked in
- You calculate achievable NOI, not theoretical NOI

**Result:** Legal compliance sets the ceiling for financial returns. Ignore it, and your model collapses when you discover half the tenants are rent-controlled and you can't raise rents 50%.

1. **Insurance Bindability Contingency:** Allows termination if carriers won't bind or cost exceeds [X% of NOI]

- ☐ Calculate "Loss-to-Lease" (difference between current rent and market rent); model capture timeline

**Title Cure Phase (Days 15-30):**

- ☐ If mechanics liens exist, obtain payoff quotes or petition to remove
- ☐ If deed restrictions limit multifamily use, contact title company about quiet title action
- ☐ If easements are problematic, contact neighbors for quitclaim or subordination
- ☐ If oil/gas reservation is active, search for current owner and obtain subordination
- ☐ Verify all Schedule B-1 requirements can be satisfied at closing (payoffs, recordings, etc.)

**Note & Deed of Trust (If Seller Financing):**

- ☐ Confirm subordination clause language; seller agrees to subordinate to future debt up to [X LTV]
- ☐ Confirm substitution of collateral clause; seller agrees to move lien if you sell property
- ☐ Specify principal-only period if planned (e.g., 36 months interest-only)
- ☐ Specify default triggers objectively (e.g., "10 days late," "property transfer without consent")
- ☐ Confirm deed of trust specifies non-judicial foreclosure (if applicable)
- ☐ Confirm both Note and Deed of Trust signed by seller; properly notarized and recorded

**Final 7 Days Before Closing:**

- ☐ Confirm all estoppels received and signed by tenants; resolve any discrepancies with seller
- ☐ Confirm all Schedule B-1 requirements satisfied (payoffs, recordings, etc.)
- ☐ Confirm insurance binder obtained and all carriers willing to bind post-closing
- ☐ Confirm all YELLOW-flag title items resolved (easements, restrictions, etc.)
- ☐ Confirm sewer scope, electrical, and roof inspections completed; any material defects escrowed
- ☐ Verify no new liens, judgments, or HOA assessments filed against seller post-commitment
- ☐ Confirm closing statement prepared; compare to LOI and purchase agreement
- ☐ Confirm deed prepared (type of deed: warranty, special warranty, quitclaim; grantor/grantee correct)
- ☐ Schedule closing; confirm all parties attending (buyer, seller, title company, possibly attorney)

## Appendix: Model Clauses & Language Templates

### Model Insurance Contingency (Comprehensive)

> **Insurance Contingency:** Buyer shall obtain binding quotations for commercial general liability (minimum $1M per occurrence, $2M aggregate), property coverage (100% replacement value), and workers' compensation (as required by state law) from a minimum of **[3]** licensed property insurers within **[14 days]** of the Effective Date. Quotations must be binding (not preliminary or "subject to inspection").
>
> (a) **Material Repair Requirements:** If any insurer requires material repairs (e.g., roof replacement, electrical rewiring, sprinkler system installation, HVAC upgrades, removal of hazardous materials) as a condition of binding coverage, Buyer shall notify Seller in writing within **[2 days]** of receiving the requirement. Seller shall have **[7 days]** to either (i) agree to fund repair escrow equal to 110% of repair estimate, (ii) perform the repairs, or (iii) waive the requirement in writing.
>
> (b) **Cost Threshold:** If the aggregate annual cost of insurance coverage exceeds **[20%]** of projected Net Operating Income (calculated as Year 1 NOI per Seller's financials), Buyer may terminate this Agreement and receive the earnest money deposit returned in full.
>
> (c) **Bindability Failure:** If any insurer declines to bind coverage or conditions coverage on exclusions Buyer deems material (e.g., exclusion of flood, wind, or liability), Buyer may terminate this Agreement.
>
> (d) **Cooperation:** Seller shall provide Buyer with prior loss history (loss runs), claims summaries, and any property condition reports in Seller's possession within **[3 days]** of Buyer's request. Seller shall make the Property available for insurer inspections.

### Model Sewer Scope & Electrical Contingency

> **Specialized Inspections Contingency:** 
>
> (a) **Sewer Scope:** Buyer shall conduct a camera inspection of the Property's sewer line from the main cleanout to the point of connection with the municipal sewer within **[21 days]** of the Effective Date. The scope shall assess for root intrusion, pipe breaks, offset joints, grease buildup, and other defects. If the scope reveals defects requiring repair (estimated cost >$[5,000]), Buyer may either (i) terminate this Agreement with earnest money refund, or (ii) require Seller to escrow **$[Repair Amount]** at closing to be released to Buyer upon completion of repairs verified by a licensed plumber and Buyer's inspector.
>
> (b) **Electrical Inspection:** Buyer shall have a licensed electrician inspect the electrical service, panel, wiring, and outlets/switches within **[21 days]** of the Effective Date. The inspection shall assess for aluminum wiring, inadequate grounding, double-tapped breakers, and code violations. If aluminum wiring is discovered, the inspection shall recommend remediation options (full rewiring, copper pigtailing, etc.). If remediation cost >$[10,000], same escrow/termination rights apply as sewer scope.
>
> (c) **Repair Escrow Release:** Any escrow holdback for repairs shall be released to Buyer upon: (i) completion of repairs; (ii) final inspection and approval by Buyer's contractor; (iii) proof of payment or lien releases from contractors; and (iv) submission of paid invoices. If repairs are not completed within **[90 days]** of closing, all escrowed funds revert to Buyer.

### Model Subordination Clause (Note)

> **Subordination:** The beneficial interest evidenced by this Deed of Trust shall be subordinate and junior to any construction loan, permanent refinancing loan, or other institutional debt ("Senior Debt") secured by the Property, provided that:
>
> (a) **Principal Cap:** The principal balance of all Senior Debt shall not exceed **[80%]** of the appraised fair market value of the Property as determined by an appraisal acceptable to Lender.
>
> (b) **Notice & Approval:** Maker shall notify Holder in writing at least **[10 business days]** before closing on any Senior Debt, providing a copy of the loan commitment letter and proposed senior deed of trust. Holder's approval shall not be unreasonably withheld.
>
> (c) **Subordination Agreement:** Maker shall ensure that Holder executes a Subordination, Non-Disturbance & Attornment (SNA) Agreement with Senior Lender, confirming Holder's subordinate position and rights to cure Senior Debt payments if Maker defaults.
>
> (d) **Payoff & Application:** Upon any payoff of this Note (through refinancing, sale, or voluntary prepayment), proceeds shall be applied as follows: (i) first, to Senior Debt; (ii) second, to this Note principal and accrued interest; (iii) third, to junior liens in order; and (iv) fourth, to Maker if surplus.

### Model Substitution of Collateral Clause

> **Substitution of Collateral:** Maker may, at Maker's sole discretion and without Holder's prior approval, substitute other real property (the "Substitute Collateral") as collateral for this Note, provided that:
>
> (a) **Value Requirement:** The Substitute Collateral shall be appraised by a licensed appraiser at Maker's expense and shall have a fair market value equal to or greater than the current Property.
>
> (b) **Cash Flow Requirement:** The Substitute Collateral shall have a Net Operating Income (NOI) equal to or greater than the current Property's NOI, calculated using Year 1 trailing twelve-month financials.
>
> (c) **Title Requirement:** The Substitute Collateral shall be free of liens and encumbrances other than property taxes, utility easements, and zoning restrictions of record.
>
> (d) **Recording:** Maker shall execute a new Deed of Trust securing this Note to the Substitute Collateral and shall record it with the appropriate county recorder. Maker shall provide Holder with a title commitment and preliminary report for the Substitute Collateral prior to substitution.
>
> (e) **Cost:** Maker shall pay all costs of substitution, including appraisal, title insurance, recording fees, and any attorney fees incurred by Holder.
>
> (f) **Notice:** Maker shall provide written notice to Holder at least **[15 days]** before substitution and shall not substitute without Holder's confirmation of receipt of the notice and all supporting documentation.

## Conclusion

This framework provides an investor acting as transaction manager with the legal tools to acquire, structure, and execute value-add multifamily deals in the 5-50 unit space. The critical success factors are:

1. **Customized PSA** with protection contingencies (insurance, inspection, estoppel) specific to vintage assets
2. **Clean owner financing papers** (Note + Deed of Trust) with subordination, substitution, and principal-only clauses enabling the creative stack
3. **Forensic title review** identifying clouds on title early and resolving them before closing
4. **Estoppel & lease audit** to verify the rent roll isn't fiction and to calculate achievable rent growth after rent control constraints
5. **Regulatory compliance mapping** that limits underwriting to achievable rent growth, tenant eviction rights, and disclosure obligations

The 2026 market rewards investors who understand these legal frameworks. Insurance hard markets no longer allow "we'll figure it out at closing." Rent control caps in CA/NY/NY require precise modeling of achievable value-add. Unsophisticated sellers demand clear, simple contracts without threatening legal language that derails negotiations.

Master these frameworks, and you'll close deals others abandon. Skip them, and you'll inherit lawsuits, insurance cancellations, and rent-controlled units that destroy IRRs.
