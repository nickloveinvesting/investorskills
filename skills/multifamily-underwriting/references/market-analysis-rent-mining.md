# MARKET ANALYSIS & RENT ROLL MINING
## A Comprehensive Underwriting Guide for Small Balance Multifamily (5-50 Units)

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This deep research report provides the forensic methodology for analyzing multifamily rent rolls to validate value-add opportunities in the small balance sector (5-50 unit properties). The core objective is to definitively prove that achievable value exists by establishing the **Loss-to-Lease**—the revenue gap between current in-place rents and market-justified rents—and then filtering that upside through the regulatory constraint of rent control legislation.

### The Central Challenge

A property acquired with rents at $1,000/month is worthless if:
1. **Market rents are actually $1,000/month** (no upside exists)
2. **Market rents are $1,400/month, but rent control caps increases at 5% + CPI** (most of the upside is blocked)
3. **The target demographic (e.g., Class C tenants earning $32,000/year) cannot afford $1,400/month** (demand does not exist)
4. **Absorption is glacial**—units take 6+ months to lease at the new price (execution risk swallows the margin)

This research guide teaches how to prove—with forensic precision—that all four conditions support the investment thesis.

### The Three Deal Killers

During market analysis and rent roll mining, most "deals" die for one of these reasons:

1. **No Loss-to-Lease** (or it's artificially inflated by overpriced "market rent" assumptions)
2. **Rent Control Legislative Ceiling** (the jurisdiction's "max allowable increase" swallows half or more of the upside)
3. **Demographic Affordability Mismatch** (the local workforce cannot support the pro forma rent, creating vacancy risk)

### What This Report Does

This guide bridges the gap between "reading a Zillow listing" and "underwriting a $3M-$8M acquisition." It covers:

- **Forensic rent roll analysis**: How to calculate Loss-to-Lease, detect economic vacancy, and spot red flags
- **Comparative Market Analysis (CMA)**: How to select and weight comps for small multifamily properties
- **Rent Control regulatory frameworks**: AB 1482 (CA), Good Cause Eviction (NY), and emerging legislation
- **Demographic validation**: Ensuring the target market can support the pro forma rent
- **Absorption rate modeling**: Predicting lease-up velocity and vacancy risk
- **The Market Rent Study template**: Creating the final deliverable document

This research assumes basic understanding of supply/demand but no prior multifamily underwriting experience.

## 1. RENT ROLL FORENSICS: THE ART OF "MINING"

### 1.1 The Rent Roll vs. The T12: The Critical Distinction

The most dangerous mistake in small multifamily underwriting is conflating the **Rent Roll** (contractual obligations) with the **T12** (actual cash received).

**The Rent Roll** shows:
- What tenants are *contractually obligated* to pay
- Unit-by-unit lease terms, rates, expiration dates
- Stated occupancy (90 units occupied, 10 vacant)

**The T12 (Trailing 12-Month Income Statement)** shows:
- What was *actually collected* over the past 12 months
- Gross Potential Rent (GPR)
- Less: Loss-to-Lease
- Less: Vacancy losses
- Less: Concessions (move-in incentives)
- Less: Bad Debt (uncollectible rent)
- = Effective Gross Income (EGI)

**Critical Rule**: A 95% "physical occupancy" rate means nothing if 15% of tenants are chronically delinquent. **Economic occupancy** (units actually paying rent) is what matters.

### 1.2 Loss-to-Lease: The Forensic Formula

Loss-to-Lease (LTL) is the single most important metric in value-add underwriting. It quantifies the monthly revenue gap between what the market would pay and what is currently being charged.

#### Standard Calculation

**Loss-to-Lease (dollar) = (Market Rent – In-Place Rent) × Number of Occupied Units**

**Example**:
- 50 units occupied at $1,000/month (current in-place rent)
- Market rent study shows comparables at $1,200/month
- Loss-to-Lease = ($1,200 – $1,000) × 50 = $10,000/month
- Annual Loss-to-Lease = $10,000 × 12 = $120,000

#### Percentage Calculation (For Per-Unit Analysis)

**Loss-to-Lease (%) = (Market Rent ÷ In-Place Rent) – 1**

**Example**:
- Market Rent: $1,200
- In-Place Rent: $1,000
- LTL % = ($1,200 ÷ $1,000) – 1 = 0.20 or 20%

This means the property is 20% below market. When the owner renovates units and re-leases them at market, they capture that 20% spread.

#### Critical Caveat: Market Rent Must Be Defensible

This is where the forensic work begins. The T12 is full of "market rent" figures that are:
- **Inflated guesses** by previous owners trying to justify aggressive projections
- **Aspirational pricing** that ignores current market conditions
- **Based on outdated comps** (not reflecting supply/demand shifts in the past 12 months)

**Your job**: Prove the market rent through independent CMA analysis (Section 2), not by accepting the seller's rent roll assertion.

### 1.3 Calculating Loss-to-Lease: A Deep Dive

#### Step 1: Determine Current In-Place Rent

Extract from the rent roll:
- Monthly rent for each occupied unit
- Calculate the average (or by unit type)

**Red Flag**: If the rent roll shows rents ranging from $800/month to $2,200/month for similar units, investigate. Wide variance suggests:
- "Family deals" (related to the owner, below-market rent)
- Long-term tenant discounts (older tenants locked in at old rates)
- Lease concessions embedded in the rent (e.g., rent shows $1,200 but actually $1,000 + 1 month free)

#### Step 2: Validate Against the T12

The T12 should show "Gross Potential Rent" (GPR) based on the rent roll. Cross-check:

**Rent Roll-Based GPR** = Average In-Place Rent × Number of Units × 12 months

**T12 GPR** = Should match (or be explained if different)

**Example of Red Flag**:
- Rent roll shows 50 units at $1,000/month average = $600,000 annual GPR
- T12 shows $900,000 GPR
- Discrepancy suggests: (a) the rent roll is outdated, (b) rents increased during the year, or (c) the T12 includes units that have since turned over

#### Step 3: Determine Market Rent (Via Independent CMA)

Do not trust the seller's "market rent" figure. Instead:

1. **Select comparable properties** (Section 2, detailed below)
2. **Adjust for amenities** (Section 2.3)
3. **Reconcile asking vs. effective rents** (Section 2.4)
4. **Calculate market rent for the subject property's unit types**

For now, assume you've determined that comparable 2-BR units in the submarket rent for $1,200/month.

#### Step 4: Calculate Loss-to-Lease by Unit Type

Small multifamily properties typically have mixed unit types. Analyze LTL separately:

| Unit Type | Units | In-Place Rent | Market Rent | LTL/Unit | Total Monthly LTL |
|-----------|-------|--------------|------------|----------|------------------|
| Studio    | 5     | $700         | $850       | $150     | $750             |
| 1-BR      | 30    | $900         | $1,150     | $250     | $7,500           |
| 2-BR      | 15    | $1,100       | $1,400     | $300     | $4,500           |
| **TOTAL** | **50**| **$920**     | **$1,176** | **$256** | **$12,750**      |

**Annual Loss-to-Lease = $12,750 × 12 = $153,000**

This is the "forced appreciation" upside available through lease rollover and renovation.

### 1.4 Detecting Economic Vacancy: The Hidden Crisis

Physical occupancy ≠ economic occupancy. The T12 must clearly delineate:

#### A. Delinquency and Bad Debt

**In the Rent Roll**, look for:
- Tenants showing "0" rent (non-paying)
- Negative balances (owing back rent)
- Aging report notation (30/60/90+ days past due)
- Eviction status

**In the T12**, this becomes:
- **Accounts Receivable (AR)** on the balance sheet
- **Bad Debt Expense** on the income statement (when written off)
- **Allowance for Doubtful Accounts** (reserve against AR)

**Rule of Thumb**: If 5%+ of rent is delinquent or written off, the property has deeper issues (tenant quality, pricing, management).

#### B. Model Units and Units Down for Renovation

The T12 may reduce GPR to account for units not generating income:
- **Model units**: Furnished, reserved for showings (typically 1-2 units in small properties)
- **Units down for renovation**: During turnover, units are not leased while being renovated and re-marketed

These are legitimate reductions, but verify they're temporary and not chronic losses.

#### C. Concessions and Rent Abatements

The rent roll may show "grossed-up" rents that are reduced by embedded concessions:

**Example**:
- Lease states: $1,200/month for 12 months = $14,400 annual rent
- Concession: 1 month free (move-in incentive)
- Effective rent: $14,400 ÷ 12 = $1,200, but tenant only pays 11 months = $13,200
- Actual monthly cash received: $13,200 ÷ 12 = $1,100

**The rent roll might show "$1,200 with 1 month concession"** while the T12 reflects the $1,100 effective rent.

**Action**: Extract all concessions from the rent roll and calculate net effective rent for each unit. Then recalculate Loss-to-Lease using effective rents, not gross rents.

### 1.5 Red Flags in the Rent Roll: "Rent Roll Fraud"

#### 1.5.1 Family Deals

When the owner's relative rents a unit significantly below market. Example:
- Owner's sister rents Unit 5 at $600/month
- Market rent for similar units: $1,200/month
- This artificially depresses the reported average rent and inflates Loss-to-Lease projections

**Detection**: Look for:
- Identical last names on tenant list and property owner
- Rents 20%+ below comparable units
- Long lease terms (5+ years) suggesting special arrangement

**Action**: Remove or normalize family-deal rents when calculating baseline occupancy metrics.

#### 1.5.2 Owner-Subsidized Units

Sometimes, owners subsidize rent for long-term tenants (effectively losing money to maintain occupancy). 

**Detection**: 
- Unit in excellent condition but rents 30%+ below comparable units
- Tenant has occupied unit for 10+ years with minimal rent increases
- T12 shows inconsistency: rent roll rent ≠ T12 income

**Impact on Underwriting**: These subsidized units will likely see tenant turnover post-acquisition (new owner, new market rent). Budget for renovation and re-leasing costs.

#### 1.5.3 Super-Long Leases with Below-Market Rents

Example: 5-year lease at $800/month signed in 2019. Market rent in 2026: $1,200/month.

**Upside Timing**: This unit is "golden"—when the lease expires in 2024, you capture $400/month upside. But you must wait; eviction (if legal) damages the business plan.

**Action**: Create a lease expiration schedule (Section 1.7) to identify when these "trapped" rents will roll.

#### 1.5.4 Concession Inflation

Heavy use of concessions (move-in incentives) is common in soft markets but masks true market rent.

**Example**:
- Advertised rent: $1,200/month
- Actual concession: 2 months free
- Effective rent: $1,200 × 10/12 = $1,000/month

**Red Flag**: If concessions are unusually high (3+ months for a 12-month lease), the stated "market rent" may be overstated. Adjust downward when calculating market assumptions.

### 1.6 The Lease Expiration Schedule: Your Value-Add Roadmap

The lease expiration schedule is the operational blueprint for value-add. It shows when units "turn" and you can implement renovations and market rent.

#### Creating the Schedule

Extract from the rent roll:
- Lease start date and lease term (e.g., 12 months)
- Calculate lease expiration date = lease start + lease term
- Sort by expiration month
- Count units expiring each month

**Example Schedule (50-unit property)**:

| Month | Units Expiring | Monthly Rent/Unit | Monthly Revenue Expiring | Renovation Plan |
|-------|----------------|-------------------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| Jan 2026   | 3              | $1,050            | $3,150                   | Immediate reno  |
| Feb 2026   | 2              | $950              | $1,900                   | Reno + wait     |
| Mar 2026   | 5              | $1,100            | $5,500                   | Reno wave 1     |
| Apr 2026   | 8              | $1,150            | $9,200                   | Reno wave 1     |
| May 2026   | 6              | $1,200            | $7,200                   | Reno wave 1     |
| Jun 2026   | 4              | $1,050            | $4,200                   | Reno wave 2     |
| Jul 2026   | 3              | $1,100            | $3,300                   | Reno wave 2     |
| Aug 2026   | 5              | $1,075            | $5,375                   | Reno wave 2     |
| **Balance (Q4 2026 + 2027)** | **14** | **$1,000** | **$14,000** | **Staggered** |

#### Critical Insights from the Schedule

1. **Seasonal Lease Expiration Pattern**: Do leases cluster in winter (bad for lease-up) or spring/summer (good)?
   - **Ideal**: Expirations spread across spring/summer (Apr-Aug), minimizing vacancy in winter.
   - **Problematic**: 30+ units expiring in January-February. This creates re-leasing challenges.

2. **Monetary Exposure**: Which months have the highest rent exposure? 
   - If your highest-rent units (2-BR) expire in January, you'll have trouble replacing them at market.

3. **Turnover Velocity**: How many units can you realistically renovate and re-lease per month?
   - Standard: 8-12 units/month for a 50-unit property (assuming 2-3 week renovation + 2-3 week marketing)
   - If 20 units expire in a single month, you'll have extended vacancy or need to hire a second crew.

### 1.7 Red Flag: The Rent Roll-to-T12 Reconciliation

**Best Practice**: Cross-reference the rent roll against the T12 income and expense statement.

**Example of What Should Match**:

| Item | Rent Roll Basis | T12 Line Item | Variance |
|------|-----------------|---------------|----------|
| Gross Potential Rent | 50 units × $1,176 avg × 12 mo = $705,600 | Market Rental Income | Should match (within 2%) |
| Loss-to-Lease | Calculated from comps | Loss-to-Lease reduction | Should be shown separately |
| Vacancy Allowance | 5 units × 12 mo = $60,000 | Vacancy Loss | Should match occupancy % |
| Concessions | 12 months free rent across all units = $14,000 | Concession reduction | Should match |
| Bad Debt | 2-3% of collectable rent | Bad Debt Expense | Review aging report |
| **Effective Gross Income** | **Sum of above** | **EGI on T12** | **Must reconcile** |

**If Reconciliation Fails**: 
- Rent roll is outdated (rents increased mid-year)
- T12 includes ancillary income not in rent roll (parking, pet fees)
- Delinquency/bad debt spiked late in the year
- Previous owner/manager made accounting errors

Request clarification from the seller's broker or accountant before proceeding.

## 2. COMPARATIVE MARKET ANALYSIS (CMA) MASTERY

### 2.1 The Core Challenge: Data Scarcity in Small Multifamily

Large institutional multifamily (150+ units) has abundant, verified data:
- CoStar and RealPage track 38M+ units nationally
- Brokers have direct access to proprietary market data
- Transactions are large enough to get broker involvement and transparency

**Small multifamily (5-50 units) is a data desert**:
- CoStar and RealPage have sparse coverage (many small buildings are "mom and pop" and don't report data)
- Zillow, Apartments.com, and Craigslist capture some data but with inconsistencies
- Most comps must be found through manual market research
- Many small buildings are owned by family offices and are not transacted frequently

**Your job**: Assemble a defensible comp set using whatever data is available, then triangulate across multiple sources to build confidence.

### 2.2 Selecting the Comp Set: The Four Dimensions

#### Dimension 1: Geographic Proximity

**Ideal**: Same submarket as subject property (within 1-2 miles)

**Rationale**: Renters make neighborhood-level decisions. A 2-BR unit 3 miles away in a "better" neighborhood is a weaker comp than an identical unit 0.5 miles away.

**For small markets** (rural Texas, for example): Expand radius to 3-5 miles if necessary to find comps.

**Red Flag Comps to Exclude**:
- Properties in fundamentally different locations (e.g., waterfront vs. inland)
- Properties serving different demographic (luxury downtown vs. workforce suburban)
- Properties with significantly different school district quality (influences family rental demand)

#### Dimension 2: Vintage and Condition Class

Properties built at different eras have different cost structures and maintenance profiles.

**Vintage Brackets**:
- **Class A** (2010+): Modern amenities, low turnover, premium rents, low capex
- **Class B** (1995-2010): Aging but well-maintained, moderate rents, moderate capex
- **Class C** (1980-1995): Dated finishes, budget rents, high capex needs (your target)
- **Class D** (Pre-1980): Deferred maintenance, lowest rents, heavy capex (distressed repositioning)

**For a pre-1990s property (Class C)**, select comps primarily from Class B-C vintage. Class A comps (new construction) will show inflated rents due to newness and should not be used.

**Example**: If your subject is built 1987, include comps from 1980-2005. Exclude 2015+ properties unless they've been extensively renovated (then they're no longer comparable).

#### Dimension 3: Unit Mix and Size

Rent is correlated with unit size. A 2-BR unit naturally rents higher than a 1-BR.

**Best Practice**:
- Separate comps into unit types: Studios, 1-BR, 2-BR, etc.
- Find 3-5 comps for each unit type
- Calculate market rent separately by type
- Apply to your subject's unit mix

**Example**:

| Unit Type | Comp 1 | Comp 2 | Comp 3 | Comp 4 | Comp 5 | Average |
|-----------|--------|--------|--------|--------|--------|---------|
| **Studio** | $750   | $780   | $770   | $765   | $775   | **$768** |
| **1-BR**   | $1,000 | $1,050 | $1,025 | $1,030 | $1,045 | **$1,030** |
| **2-BR**   | $1,400 | $1,450 | $1,425 | $1,440 | $1,460 | **$1,435** |

Apply this to your subject property's unit mix to calculate blended market rent.

#### Dimension 4: Building Amenities

Do not use properties with fundamentally different amenities (e.g., luxury swimming pool, fitness center, rooftop deck) as a primary comp. Instead, use the adjustment matrix (Section 2.3).

**Guidance**:
- Subject: Walk-up, no pool, limited parking = use walk-up comps as primary set
- Subject: Garden-style, community pool, fitness center = find similar garden-style comps with comparable amenities

### 2.3 The Amenity Adjustment Matrix: Pricing Specific Features

This is where forensic CMA becomes art and science. You must assign a **dollar value** to specific amenities that influence rent.

#### Step 1: Identify Baseline Comp (No Adjustments)

Select one comparable property that most closely matches the subject in location, vintage, and unit mix. This is your **baseline property**, to which all other comps are adjusted.

**Example Baseline**:
- Location: Submarket ZIP code
- Vintage: 1987 (matches subject)
- Unit Type: 2-BR, 1-BA
- Amenities: Walk-up, no pool, on-site laundry (shared)
- Asking Rent: $1,300/month

#### Step 2: Build the Adjustment Schedule

For each comparable, calculate adjustments based on specific amenities present.

**Sample Amenity Adjustment Values** (Evidence-Based Ranges for Class B/C Properties):

| Amenity | Adjustment | Source / Notes |
|---------|------------|---|
| **In-Unit Laundry** | +$50 to +$100/mo | High convenience factor; payback period 12-18 months |
| **Central AC** | +$50 to +$75/mo | Essential in hot climates; may be standard in some markets |
| **Balcony/Patio** | +$25 to +$50/mo | Modest premium; outdoor space preference |
| **Community Pool** | +$40 to +$80/mo | Seasonal value; higher in warm climates; lower demand in winter |
| **Fitness Center** | +$20 to +$50/mo | Lower ROI than laundry; millennial preference |
| **Covered Parking** | +$30 to +$75/mo | Market and weather dependent |
| **Updated Kitchen** | +$50 to +$150/mo | Highly visible renovation; strong ROI |
| **Hardwood/LVP Flooring** | +$40 to +$100/mo | Vs. older carpet; durability + aesthetic |
| **Granite Countertops** | +$30 to +$75/mo | Upgrade perception |
| **High-Speed Internet (Included)** | +$10 to +$30/mo | Increasingly expected, not a major differentiator |
| **Pet-Friendly** | +$15 to +$50/mo | Plus pet fees (one-time or monthly) |
| **Proximity to Transit** | +$30 to +$100/mo | Market dependent; major factor in urban areas |

#### Step 3: Apply Adjustments to Calculate Adjusted Rents

**Example Calculation**:

**Baseline Comp** (no adjustments):
- 2-BR unit, $1,300/month

**Comparable #1** (vs. baseline):
- Asking rent: $1,350/month
- Has: In-unit laundry (+$75), updated kitchen (+$80), covered parking (+$50)
- Total positive adjustments: +$205
- **Adjusted rent for comparison**: $1,350 – $205 = **$1,145** (this comp is overpriced relative to the baseline)

**Comparable #2** (vs. baseline):
- Asking rent: $1,250/month
- Missing: Central AC (–$50), older kitchen (–$60)
- Total negative adjustments: –$110
- **Adjusted rent for comparison**: $1,250 – (–$110) = $1,250 + $110 = **$1,360** (this comp is underpriced relative to the baseline; correcting for differences shows it's actually worth more)

#### Step 4: Reconcile the Adjusted Rent Range

After adjusting multiple comps:

| Comparable | Adjusted Rent |
|------------|---------------|
| Comp 1     | $1,145        |
| Comp 2     | $1,360        |
| Comp 3     | $1,280        |
| Comp 4     | $1,310        |
| Comp 5     | $1,275        |
| **Average** | **$1,274** |
| **Range**  | **$1,145 – $1,360** |

This range is your defensible market rent estimate for 2-BR units. 

**Conservative approach**: Use the **lower end of the range** ($1,145) as your market rent assumption.
**Aggressive approach**: Use the **average** ($1,274).
**Optimistic approach**: Use the **high end** ($1,360).

**Best Practice**: Run sensitivity analysis (Section 6) on all three, showing underwriting outcomes at bear, base, and bull cases.

### 2.4 Asking Rent vs. Achieved Rent vs. Effective Rent

These terms are often conflated. Precision matters.

#### Definition and Hierarchy

**Asking Rent** (Advertised Rent):
- The rent listed on Apartments.com, Zillow, Craigslist
- What the landlord *wants* to charge
- Often inflated during market peaks; may not reflect actual lease signings

**Achieved Rent** (Signed Rent):
- The rent actually signed in the lease *before* concessions
- If lease says "$1,200/month," achieved rent = $1,200
- Still does not account for move-in incentives

**Effective Rent** (Net Effective Rent):
- The average monthly rent *after* concessions are amortized across the lease term
- Formula: (Gross Rent – Total Concessions) / # of Lease Months
- Example: $1,200 lease with 1 month free = ($1,200 × 12 – $1,200) / 12 = $1,100 effective rent

#### The Concession Problem in Soft Markets

In weak markets (2025-2026 in many submarkets), concessions are rampant:

- **37.3% of new leases include some concession** (2025 data)
- **Average concession**: 5-8 weeks of free rent in stabilized properties
- High-supply markets (Austin, Denver, Nashville, Phoenix): 6+ weeks of free rent common

**Impact on CMA**:

If you pull comparables from online listings showing $1,400/month, but the reality includes 2 months free rent:
- Effective rent = $1,400 × 10/12 = $1,167/month

**Action**: When gathering comp data, always ask:
1. What is the **asking rent** (advertised)?
2. What **concessions** are currently being offered?
3. What is the **effective rent** after concessions?

Calculate effective rent for all comps before building your adjustment matrix.

#### CoStar Data Fields

CoStar (the institutional data provider) distinguishes:
- **Asking Rent**: Listed price
- **Rent Achieved**: Signed lease rent (pre-concessions)
- **Effective Rent**: Post-concession average

For small multifamily, CoStar coverage is sparse. Use Zillow, Apartments.com, and boots-on-ground research, then manually calculate effective rents.

### 2.5 Data Sources for Small Multifamily Comps

#### Primary Data Sources (Ranked by Reliability)

1. **CoStar**
   - **Pros**: Comprehensive verified data, 38M+ units, institutional standard
   - **Cons**: Expensive ($5K-50K/year for subscriptions); small multifamily coverage is poor
   - **Use Case**: If you have institutional backing or subscription access

2. **Direct Market Research (Boots-on-Ground)**
   - **Method**: Tour comparable properties, speak with leasing agents, ask about current rent, concessions, occupancy
   - **Pros**: First-hand verification; can ask pointed questions
   - **Cons**: Time-intensive; requires local market presence
   - **Use Case**: Essential for small multifamily; supplement with online sources

3. **Zillow Rent Zestimate & Rental Manager**
   - **Pros**: Free rent estimates for 125M+ homes; integrates comps and market trends
   - **Cons**: Algorithm-based (not transparent); accuracy varies by market
   - **Use Case**: Quick sanity check; starting point for comp range

4. **Apartments.com & Zillow Rental Listings**
   - **Pros**: Real-time asking rents; large sample size
   - **Cons**: Selection bias (occupied units not listed); does not include concessions until you call
   - **Use Case**: Primary online data source for small multifamily

5. **Rentometer**
   - **Pros**: Quick rent estimates by address or ZIP; comparables database
   - **Cons**: Limited to basic unit types (beds/baths); sparse data for smaller markets
   - **Use Case**: Secondary verification tool

6. **Craigslist & Facebook Marketplace**
   - **Pros**: Local listings from small landlords; includes for-rent-by-owner units
   - **Cons**: No verification; asking rents only (not achieved); volatile sample
   - **Use Case**: Additional data points; local flavor

#### Assembling the Data

**Standard CMA Process for a 20-Unit, 1-BR/2-BR Property**:

1. **CoStar or online search**: Filter for 5-30 unit properties, 1980-2000 vintage, 1-BR and 2-BR units, same ZIP or adjacent ZIP
   - Target: 20-30 candidate properties

2. **Screen candidates**: Eliminate if:
   - Significantly different amenities (luxury vs. workforce)
   - Different demographic service area
   - Non-standard unit mix (all studios, or all 3-BR)
   - Likely student housing or subsidized affordable housing

3. **Drive-by visual inspection**: Verify property condition matches rental pricing expectations
   - A property advertising $1,200/mo should look consistent with a property at that price point
   - If it's visibly deteriorated, it's not a valid comp (disconnect signals data error)

4. **Call or visit**: Speak with leasing office
   - Current asking rent for 1-BR and 2-BR
   - Current occupancy rate
   - Concessions offered (free months, move-in specials)
   - Lease terms
   - Amenities included

5. **Document**: Create spreadsheet with all data
   - Address, vintage, unit count (if known)
   - Asking rent by type
   - Concessions (specify: $X/month discount, X free months, waived deposits, etc.)
   - Occupancy and comments (e.g., "all units leased, no concessions")
   - Calculate effective rent

6. **Calculate adjusted rents**: Apply amenity adjustment matrix
   - Subject has: no in-unit laundry, window AC, no balcony, on-site pool
   - Comp A has: in-unit laundry (+$75), central AC (+$50), balcony (+$35)
   - Adjustment: –$160 (Comp A is better, so its rent must be adjusted down to be comparable)

7. **Reconcile range**: Average adjusted rents; identify outliers
   - If one comp is 15%+ above/below others, investigate whether:
     - Data error (call and verify)
     - Legitimate difference (e.g., comp is in premium location, or is mixed-income affordable housing)

## 3. NAVIGATING THE REGULATORY MINEFIELD: RENT CONTROL & COMPLIANCE

This section is the make-or-break component. A property with 30% Loss-to-Lease is worthless if the law caps annual increases at 2%.

### 3.1 Overview: The Rent Control Landscape in 2025-2026

**State of Play**:
- **California**: AB 1482 (statewide rent cap: 5% + CPI, capped at 10%) in effect since Jan 1, 2020; applies to most multifamily units built pre-2005
- **New York**: Good Cause Eviction law (effective April 2024); rent cap formula: 5% + inflation, capped at 10% (currently ~8.79% for 2025)
- **Other states**: 40+ states have no statewide rent control; patchwork of local ordinances (Oregon, Minneapolis, some East Coast cities)
- **2026 Proposal**: California AB 1157 would lower the cap to 2% + inflation (capped at 5%), extending coverage to single-family homes

### 3.2 California AB 1482: The Default Reference

**Scope**: Applies to residential rental properties in California that:
- Were built at least 15 years ago (pre-2009 as of 2024; rolling basis)
- Are NOT owned by an individual who owns only 1-2 units (single-family/duplex exemption)
- Are NOT subject to a local rent control ordinance that is more restrictive

**Key Provisions**:

#### Rent Increase Limitations

Annual rent increases are limited to the **lower of**:
- 5% + the regional Consumer Price Index (CPI), or
- 10%

**Current Rates (2025-2026, effective August 2025 – July 2026)**:

| Region | CPI (%) | Max Increase |

#### Just-Cause Eviction

Landlords cannot evict tenants without "just cause" (e.g., non-payment, lease violation, owner move-in). This does not directly impact rent, but prevents displacing tenants who refuse post-lease-expiration rent increases.

#### Key Exemptions

Properties **exempt from both rent increase caps and just-cause**:
- New construction (built within the last 15 years; rolling exemption)
- Single-family homes or condominiums owned by an individual (not a corporation/REIT/LLC with corporate member); requires written notice to tenant
- Owner-occupied duplexes (one unit occupied by owner)
- Properties subject to a local ordinance more restrictive than AB 1482
- Affordable housing (LIHTC, project-based vouchers, etc.)

#### No Limit on Initial Rent for Vacant Units

Critical exemption: When a unit turns over and becomes vacant, the landlord can re-lease it at any rent. No cap.

**This is the value-add loophole**: When a tenant moves out, you can renovate and re-lease at market. The 5% + CPI cap applies to **renewals** (same tenant staying), not new leases.

**Example**:
- Current tenant has lease at $1,100/month (below market)
- Unit is vacant
- You renovate and re-lease to new tenant at $1,400/month (market rate)
- Legal ✓ (no rent cap on vacant units)

- But if current tenant refuses to leave, and you try to evict for "rent increase too high":
- Legal ✗ (just-cause eviction prevents non-payment or no-fault evictions based solely on rent increase)

**Implication for Underwriting**: Your value-add model relies on **lease turnover**. Units that are currently occupied will see capped increases (5% + CPI) when renewed. Vacant units can be re-leased at market.

### 3.3 New York Good Cause Eviction Law

**Scope**: Applies to market-rate (non-stabilized) apartments in New York State; effective April 20, 2024

**Rent Increase Limitations**:
- Local rent standard: 5% + inflation (CPI), capped at 10%
- For 2025 (effective through Feb 2026): **8.79%** (rounded from 5% + 3.79% CPI)
- This standard is set annually; watch for 2026 update

**Exemptions**:
- Rent-stabilized apartments (already subject to NYC Rent Stabilization Ordinance; separate, stricter caps)
- High-rent units (>245% of Fair Market Rent; currently $6,004+ for 1-BR in NYC)
- Small landlords (own ≤10 units in NY; applies per-person if multiple owners)
- Sublease situations (where sublessor seeks to recover unit)
- Institutional units (hospitals, dormitories, hotels, religious facilities)

**Nuance**: Unlike California AB 1482, **no exemption for vacant units**. A landlord cannot re-lease a vacant unit above the "local rent standard" – they must comply with the 8.79% (or current year's standard) increase.

This significantly limits value-add in New York (vs. California).

### 3.4 Creating the "Go/No-Go" Decision Tree

Use this framework to assess whether rent control **allows** the business plan.

#### Step 1: Determine the Jurisdiction's Legal Regime

**Question**: Is the property subject to state-level or local rent control?

- California: Check if property is 15+ years old and not already subject to stricter local control
- New York: Check if market-rate or stabilized; if market-rate, subject to Good Cause Eviction
- Other states: Check for local rent control ordinance (city or county level)

**Output**: Identify the applicable **rent cap formula** and any exemptions.

#### Step 2: Determine Rent Increase Allowance for the Investment Holding Period

**Question**: Over the 5-7 year holding period, what total rent increase is legally permissible?

**Example (California, Alameda County)**:
- Year 1 (2025-26): 6.8% allowed
- Year 2 (2026-27): Assume 6.5% (assuming CPI declines)
- Year 3 (2027-28): Assume 6.5%
- Year 4-5: Assume 6.0%
- **Cumulative after 5 years**: ~33% total increase

**Example (New York, Good Cause)**:
- Year 1-5: Assume 8% annual (averaging across CPI fluctuations)
- **Cumulative after 5 years**: ~46% total increase (note: no vacant unit exemption, so this applies to all units)

#### Step 3: Stress-Test Against Loss-to-Lease

**Question**: Does the rent control cap allow you to capture the Loss-to-Lease?

**Example**:
- Subject property: 50 units at $1,000/month average
- Market rent: $1,200/month
- Loss-to-Lease: $200/unit/month = 20%

**Scenario A (California)**: 
- Lease expiration in Year 3 (2027-28)
- Tenant renewal increase allowed: 6.5%
- New rent: $1,000 × 1.065 = $1,065/month
- **You capture only $65/month of the $200/month upside**
- New tenant re-lease: $1,200/month (vacant unit exemption applies)
- **Result**: Lease turnover is critical to capturing full upside

**Scenario B (New York)**:
- Lease expiration in Year 3 (2027-28)
- Tenant renewal increase allowed: ~8% (Good Cause standard)
- New rent: $1,000 × 1.08 = $1,080/month
- **You capture only $80/month of the $200/month upside**
- New tenant re-lease: Still capped at 8% (no vacant unit exemption in NY)
- New rent: $1,000 × 1.08 = $1,080/month
- **You cannot legally achieve $1,200/month even with vacant unit**

#### Step 4: Model Lease Turnover Timeline

This is the lynchpin. When will units turn so you can re-lease at market?

**Use the lease expiration schedule (Section 1.7)**:

| Year | Units Expiring | Avg In-Place Rent | Market Rent | Realized Capture |
|------|----------------|-------------------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1    | 12             | $1,000            | $1,200      | 100% @ market   |
| 2    | 10             | $1,030            | $1,224      | 100% @ market   |
| 3    | 13             | $1,062            | $1,249      | 100% @ market   |
| 4    | 9              | $1,096            | $1,277      | 100% @ market   |
| 5    | 6              | $1,132            | $1,306      | 100% @ market   |
| **Renewal (ongoing)** | **Variable** | Increased/renewing | Market (capped) | 5-8% increase |

**Analysis**: In Year 1-5, as units turn, you capture market rent (or nearly so). Remaining tenants see capped increases (5-8%). By Year 6+, most units have turned and are at market. Capped increases apply to renewals.

#### Step 5: Calculate IRR Sensitivity to Rent Cap Scenarios

Run three scenarios:

1. **Base Case**: Assumed rent cap (e.g., 6.8% annually); capture Loss-to-Lease as scheduled
2. **Bear Case**: Rent cap tightens (e.g., legislation passes; cap drops to 3% + CPI)
3. **Bull Case**: Exemption/waiver (e.g., substantial rehabilitation exemption applies; no cap)

**IRR Impact**:
- Base Case: 12-15% IRR
- Bear Case: 8-10% IRR (still acceptable for some investors)
- Bull Case: 18-20% IRR (potential upside if legal exemption applies)

**Threshold**: If Bear Case IRR drops below target (e.g., 8%), the deal becomes risky.

### 3.5 Legal Methods to Maximize Revenue Within Rent Caps

Even with strict rent control, operators have tools:

#### A. Resident Reimbursable Utilities (RUBS)

**What it is**: Instead of bundling utilities into rent, tenants pay for utilities proportionally (based on unit size, occupancy, etc.), separate from base rent.

**Benefit**: Utility charges are **not counted as rent** for rent control purposes; therefore, rent caps do not apply.

**Example (California, with 6.8% rent cap)**:
- Current rent: $1,000/month
- Current utilities (bundled in rent): $150/month
- Rent cap increase: $1,000 × 1.068 = $1,068/month

- **With RUBS**:
  - Base rent increase: $1,000 × 1.068 = $1,068/month
  - Utility reimbursement: $150/month (no cap)
  - **Total tenant payment: $1,218/month**

**Caveat**: Not legal everywhere. In some California jurisdictions (e.g., Los Angeles), RUBS is heavily regulated and the utility charges may be counted toward the rent cap. Verify local legality before implementation.

**Operational Friction**:
- Must provide 6 months' advance notice to tenants
- Requires energy audit to establish fair allocation formula
- Tenants may object or face payment resistance
- Implementation costs: ~$500-1,000 per unit

#### B. Capital Improvement Pass-Throughs (NY Specific)

In New York, Good Cause Eviction allows for **major capital improvement** rent surcharges.

**How it works**:
- Owner invests in building-wide improvements (e.g., new roof, HVAC system, windows)
- Owner petitions the Rent Board to pass portion of costs to tenants via rent surcharge
- Surcharge is separate from the standard annual increase

**Timing**: Typically, surcharges can be collected for 10 years or until costs are recovered.

**Example**:
- Owner spends $500,000 on roof replacement (50-unit building = $10,000/unit)
- Owner files for 10-year amortization: $10,000 ÷ 10 = $1,000/unit/year surcharge
- Tenant's base rent increases 8% (Good Cause standard)
- Tenant's rent also includes $83/month surcharge
- **Total increase**: 8% + ~$83 = effective 15%+ (depending on base rent)

**Limitation**: Only applies in NY; requires formal Rent Board approval; and tenants can challenge if improvements don't benefit all units.

#### C. "Banking" Unused Annual Increases

Many rent control laws allow landlords to "bank" unused increases and apply them in future years.

**How it works**:
- Annual cap: 5% + CPI = 6% (example)
- Owner grants renewal: 3% (below cap)
- "Banked" increase: 6% – 3% = 3%
- Next year, owner can increase up to 6% + 3% (banked) = 9% (if tenant renews)

**Caveat**: Only applies if law explicitly allows banking; varies by jurisdiction. Check local statutes.

#### D. "Substantial Rehabilitation" Exemption (NY, CA Historic Properties)

Properties that undergo "substantial rehabilitation" may be exempt from rent control entirely.

**New York Definition**:
- Building must be in seriously deteriorated condition (≥80% of units vacant, or other indicators)
- Owner conducts major renovation (≥50% of building costs over 24 months, or 125% replacement cost, or other triggers)
- Owner must document all work, costs, permits, before/after photos

**California (Historic Tax Credit)**:
- Historic properties (pre-1936 or listed on NRHP) with certified rehabilitation may qualify for federal tax credit
- State law may provide rent control exemption for certified historic rehabs

**Implication for Underwriting**:
- If subject property is pre-1920s historic and slated for major rehab, investigate whether substantial rehabilitation exemption applies
- If yes, you may be able to re-lease renovated units with **no rent cap**
- This dramatically improves IRR

**Caveat**: Very difficult to qualify; requires extensive documentation and DHCR/Rent Board approval in NY. Do not assume exemption without legal counsel review.

### 3.6 Red Flag: Local Ordinances More Restrictive Than State Law

California AB 1482 allows cities to impose stricter rent control. Examples:

- **San Francisco**: 1.4% annual increase cap (2025-26) – much stricter than 6.8% state standard
- **Los Angeles**: 3% annual increase (2025) – stricter than LA County's 7.5%
- **West Hollywood, Oakland, Berkeley, Santa Monica**: All have local caps stricter than state baseline

**Impact on Underwriting**:
- A property in San Francisco has 1.4% annual cap (vs. 6.8% in much of CA)
- Loss-to-Lease cannot be captured through rent increases
- Value-add relies entirely on lease turnover (vacant unit re-leasing)
- If lease expiration schedule shows expirations spread over 7+ years, value-add timeline is extended (and riskier)

**Action**: Always verify which **local rent control ordinance** applies to the subject property, not just state law.

## 4. DEMOGRAPHIC FEASIBILITY & ABSORPTION

Establishing Loss-to-Lease and confirming rent control allows it are necessary but not sufficient. You must also prove the market **can and will** pay the target rent.

### 4.1 Rent-to-Income Ratio: The Hard Ceiling

The industry standard is: **Tenants spend 30% of gross income on rent.**

This is enforced by:
- Landlord screening (require income ≥3x rent)
- Tenant affordability analysis (HUD Fair Market Rent benchmarks)
- Government housing voucher payment standards (Section 8)

**Example**:
- Target market rent: $1,400/month
- Tenant income requirement: $1,400 ÷ 0.30 = $4,667/month gross income
- Annual income: $56,000+

#### Class C Tenant Income Reality Check

Class C multifamily serves lower-income renters. National data (2022):
- **Class A**: Median rent-to-income = 20.5%
- **Class B**: Median rent-to-income = 22.1%
- **Class C**: Median rent-to-income = 24.5%

**Note**: Class C tenants spend a *higher* percentage of income on rent, not a lower percentage. This is because absolute income is lower, so the same rent consumes more of their budget.

**Implication**: If local Class C median household income is $38,000/year, and you propose to increase rents from $1,000 to $1,400/month:
- Current rent-to-income: $1,000/month × 12 ÷ $38,000 = 31.6% (already at/above affordability ceiling)
- Proposed rent-to-income: $1,400/month × 12 ÷ $38,000 = 44.2% (**unaffordable**)
- **Result**: Market cannot support $1,400 rent at this income level

### 4.2 Demographic Analysis Workflow

#### Step 1: Define the Target Demographic

Based on the property's location, type, and current tenant profile, identify:
- **Age**: Family (25-45), Young Professional (22-35), or Retiree (55+)?
- **Income Level**: Class A (>$100k household), Class B ($60-100k), or Class C (<$60k)?
- **Employment Sector**: Professional, service industry, blue-collar, government?
- **Household Composition**: Singles, couples, families with children?

#### Step 2: Research Local Median Income and Employment

Use U.S. Census Bureau data (American Community Survey) for the ZIP code or metro area:
- **Median Household Income**
- **Per-Capita Income**
- **Unemployment Rate**
- **Major Employers**

**Example (Little Elm, TX, where user is located)**:
- Median Household Income (ACS 2023): ~$105,000
- Per-Capita Income: ~$45,000
- Unemployment: 3.2%
- Growth rate: +15% population growth (2020-2023)
- Major employers: Tech companies (migrating from California), healthcare, retail

#### Step 3: Calculate Maximum "Affordable" Rent by Income Cohort

Using the 30% rule:

| Income Cohort | Annual Household Income | Max Affordable Rent (30%) |
|---------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Bottom 20% | $30,000 | $750/month |
| 21-40% | $50,000 | $1,250/month |

| 61-80% | $90,000 | $2,250/month |
| Top 20% | $120,000+ | $3,000+/month |

#### Step 4: Validate Market Rent Assumption Against Local Income

**Critical Check**: Does the pro forma market rent align with actual local income?

**Red Flag Example**:
- Subject property in rural area with median household income $40,000
- Pro forma market rent: $1,400/month
- Rent-to-income required: $56,000+ household income
- **Reality**: Market cannot sustain $1,400 rent; out of reach for 80%+ of local renters

**Resolution Options**:
1. **Lower the rent target**: Use $1,000/month as market rent (supported by $40,000 household income)
2. **Redefine the demographic**: If you're targeting in-migration of higher-income residents (job growth), adjust income assumptions upward
3. **Accept smaller upside**: If market rent is $1,200 (vs. $1,400 pro forma), Loss-to-Lease shrinks, but deal may still pencil

### 4.3 Absorption Rate: The Lease-Up Velocity Challenge

Even if the market *can* pay the rent, will tenants actually lease units at that price *quickly*?

#### Definition

**Absorption Rate** = (Net Absorption ÷ Available Inventory) × 100

Where:
- **Net Absorption** = Units leased – Units vacated in a given period
- **Available Inventory** = Vacant units + Space for lease

**Example**:
- Market had 100 vacant apartments (start of period)
- New construction added 50 units
- 80 units were leased
- 30 units vacated
- Net Absorption = 80 – 30 = 50 units
- Absorption Rate = (50 ÷ 150) × 100 = 33% per period

#### Absorption Rate Benchmarks (U.S. Multifamily, 2025-2026)

- **Strong market**: 2%+ per month (24%+ annually) – fast lease-up, rising rents
- **Balanced market**: 1-2% per month – stable, competitive leasing
- **Soft market**: <1% per month – slow lease-up, concessions needed
- **Negative absorption**: More units vacating than leasing – oversupply, downward rent pressure

**2025-2026 Market Snapshot**:
- After 2024 peak supply, absorption is declining
- Q1 2025: 102,000 units leased vs. 95,000 delivered (slight positive absorption)
- Expectations for 2026: Absorption may drop to pre-pandemic norms (slower, but still positive)

- Extend holding period/refinance timeline to accommodate slower ramp

## 5. UTILITY & EXPENSE BILL-BACK VIABILITY (RUBS)

### 5.1 RUBS as a Value-Add Strategy

Many pre-1990s multifamily buildings have **central metering** for utilities—all units share one meter, and the owner pays one bill and absorbs the cost.

**Problem**: Utilities are bundled into rent, and the owner has no visibility into actual tenant consumption. Costs rise with CPI but are locked in by rent control caps.

**Solution**: Implement **Ratio Utility Billing System (RUBS)** to pass utility costs to tenants.

#### RUBS Mechanics

Instead of submetering each unit (capital-intensive: $750-2,000 per meter × 50 units = $37,500-100,000), RUBS allocates the building's total utility bill to tenants using a formula:

**RUBS Formula Options**:
- **Square Footage**: Utility costs ÷ total building SF × unit's SF = unit's share
- **Occupancy**: Utility costs ÷ number of tenants × unit's occupancy = unit's share
- **Hybrid**: Combination of SF (for heating/cooling) and occupancy (for water/sewer)

**Example**:
- Building annual utility bill: $60,000
- Total building SF: 40,000
- Unit A SF: 800 (2%)
- Unit A RUBS charge: $60,000 × (800÷40,000) = $1,200/year = $100/month

#### Financial Impact on Multifamily Operator

**Before RUBS**:
- Rent: $1,000/month (includes utilities)
- Owner's utility cost: ~$120/month per unit average
- Owner's effective rent: $880/month

**After RUBS**:
- Base rent: $1,000/month
- Tenant's RUBS charge: ~$120/month
- Owner's revenue: $1,120/month
- Owner's utility cost: $0 (passed to tenants)
- **Net benefit**: $120/month per unit = $1,440/year per unit

**For a 50-unit property**: $1,440 × 50 = $72,000/year incremental NOI

#### RUBS ROI Calculation

**Implementation costs** (per unit):
- Legal review & lease amendment: $100-200
- Utility audit & formula design: $500-1,000 per property (one-time)
- Tenant notice & education: $100-200
- Billing system integration: $200-500

**Per-unit cost**: ~$500-1,500 (one-time)

**Payback period**: $1,440/year ÷ $1,000 per unit = ~1 year

### 5.2 Legal Status and Tenant Reception of RUBS

#### Legal Landscape

**California (Including LA, SF, and Other Cities)**:
- RUBS is legal statewide
- **Critical caveat**: In rent-controlled jurisdictions (LA, SF), RUBS charges may be counted as part of "rent" for purposes of the rent cap
  - Example: LA allows 3% rent increase + RUBS charges together must not exceed 3% of total rent + utilities
  - This means RUBS does NOT escape the rent cap; it just shifts where costs are paid
  - Verification required: Check LA Housing Department and SF Rent Board guidance

**New York**:
- RUBS is legal
- Good Cause Eviction law does not mention RUBS; check NYC Housing Preservation Department and local management guidelines

**Texas**:
- RUBS is legal; no statewide rent control
- No regulatory restrictions

#### Tenant Reception and Implementation Challenges

**Pros**:
- Encourages conservation (tenants see direct incentive to reduce consumption)
- Transparent (utilities are separately itemized, not bundled in rent)
- Predictable billing (monthly RUBS charges are calculated, not variable with weather)

**Cons**:
- Tenants may perceive it as a "hidden rent increase"
  - Example: Tenant expects $1,000/month rent, but suddenly pays $1,000 + $120 RUBS
  - Dissatisfaction can drive turnover
- If implemented poorly (without advance notice), tenants may refuse payment or break leases

#### Best Practices for Implementation

1. **Timing**: Implement RUBS when leases renew (not mid-lease)
2. **Notice**: Provide 6-12 months' advance written notice to tenants
3. **Education**: Hold tenant meetings, provide examples of calculations, explain benefits (utility conservation)
4. **Phase-in (optional)**: Gradually increase RUBS charges over 2-3 years (reduce shock)
5. **Audit**: Commission independent energy audit; present formula as "fair allocation based on usage patterns"
6. **Transparency**: Provide monthly RUBS statements showing building total bill and unit allocation

### 5.3 RUBS in the Rent Roll Mining Analysis

When analyzing a property's current financials:

**Question**: Is the current owner already implementing RUBS?

- **If yes**: T12 operating expenses will show low utility costs (tenants paying). Rent figures may be stated "plus RUBS" or separately.
  - Action: Verify RUBS is properly implemented and legally documented. Plan to continue post-acquisition.

- **If no**: T12 operating expenses include full utility bill (owner-subsidized).
  - Action: Calculate opportunity for RUBS implementation. Model incremental NOI benefit post-acquisition.

**Example Rent Roll Analysis**:

**Current (Without RUBS)**:
- GPR (Gross Potential Rent): $600,000/year
- Utilities (owner-paid): $60,000/year
- NOI (before other expenses): $540,000/year

**Post-Acquisition (With RUBS)**:
- GPR: $600,000/year
- RUBS pass-through: $60,000/year (charged to tenants)
- Utilities (owner-paid): $0 (passed through)
- NOI (incremental): +$60,000/year

This incremental $60,000 is "forced appreciation" from operational optimization, separate from Loss-to-Lease rent increases.

## 6. IMPLEMENTATION: THE MARKET RENT STUDY TEMPLATE

A professional **Market Rent Study** is the final deliverable document that proves the deal is viable. It should be defensible, concise, and suitable for presentation to lenders and co-investors.

### 6.1 Market Rent Study Outline and Contents

#### I. Executive Summary (1 page)
- Property address and basics (age, units, location)
- Current average rent vs. market rent
- Loss-to-Lease opportunity (dollar and percentage)
- Key assumptions (holding period, lease turnover rate, rent growth)
- Conclusion: Achievable rent increase and timeline

#### II. Property Overview (1-2 pages)
- Physical description (vintage, construction type, unit mix)
- Current occupancy and tenant profile
- Current rent by unit type (from rent roll)
- Current occupancy rate and lease expiration schedule
- Photos of subject property (interior/exterior)

#### III. Comparable Properties Analysis (2-3 pages)
- List of 4-6 comparable properties selected
  - Address, age, unit count, unit mix
  - Current asking rent, effective rent (accounting for concessions)
  - Amenities, condition assessment
- Adjustment matrix showing calculations
- Adjusted rent reconciliation
- **Conclusion: Market rent range** (e.g., $1,145 – $1,360 for 2-BR units)

#### IV. Loss-to-Lease Calculation (1 page)
- Current rent by unit type
- Market rent (from CMA) by unit type
- Loss-to-Lease dollar and percentage
- Annualized value of opportunity
- Timeline for capturing (lease expiration schedule)

#### V. Regulatory Analysis (1-2 pages)
- Applicable rent control law (AB 1482 CA, Good Cause NY, etc.)
- Maximum allowable annual increase
- Exemptions applicable to subject property (if any)
- Conclusion: Whether and how the target market rent can be legally achieved

#### VI. Demographic Feasibility (1 page)
- Target tenant demographic (age, income, employment)
- Local median household income (Census data)
- Rent-to-income analysis: Is target market rent affordable?
- Major employers and job growth trends
- Conclusion: Demand supports target rent

#### VII. Absorption and Lease-Up Analysis (1 page)
- Local market absorption rate (monthly or annual)
- Historical absorption for this property type/vintage in market
- Projected lease-up timeline (months to stabilization)
- Seasonal considerations (peak vs. slow seasons)
- Conclusion: Realistic lease-up velocity supports plan

#### VIII. Value-Add Opportunities Beyond Rent (0.5 page)
- Renovation scope (cosmetic vs. structural)
- Capital improvements that justify rent premium
  - E.g., new kitchen (+$50-150/mo), in-unit laundry (+$50-100/mo)
- Ancillary revenue (RUBS, parking, pet fees, etc.)
- Total value-add opportunity

#### IX. Investment Underwriting Summary (1 page)
- Pro forma revenue (stabilized year)
  - In-place rent + Loss-to-Lease capture + RUBS
  - Occupancy assumption (e.g., 92%)
  - Effective Gross Income
- Pro forma expenses (stabilized)
  - Property management, utilities (post-RUBS), maintenance, reserves
  - NOI estimate
- Valuation
  - Stabilized NOI × Cap Rate = Stabilized Value
  - Example: $500K NOI ÷ 6.0% = $8.33M value
- IRR and equity multiple
  - Purchase price, renovation costs, financing
  - 5-7 year hold period
  - Exit value and returns

- **T12 Income Statement** (last 12 months)

- **Comparable Properties Data Sheet**
- **Local Market Data** (employment, population growth, income statistics)

### 6.2 Writing the Market Rent Study: Key Principles

#### 1. Defensibility Over Aggressiveness

Every number must be traceable to a source:
- Market rents: cite comp properties (addresses, leasing confirmation dates)
- Loss-to-Lease percentages: show calculation
- Absorption rates: cite CoStar, RealPage, or local broker data (or note "analyst estimate")
- Regulatory caps: cite statute or city ordinance

**Avoid**:
- Vague language ("market is strong")
- Unsupported claims ("rent should be $1,400")
- Conflating asking rent with market rent

**Example of Weak Language**:
> "Market rent is estimated at $1,400 based on general market conditions."

**Example of Strong Language**:
> "Market rent is estimated at $1,400 based on comparable analysis of 5 similar Class B 2-bedroom units (Comps A-E, see Appendix) built 1990-2005, within 1.5 miles, after adjusting for amenities (in-unit laundry +$75, upgraded kitchen +$80, covered parking +$50). Average adjusted rent: $1,274; conservative estimate: $1,200; optimistic estimate: $1,360. For underwriting, we use base case of $1,200."

#### 2. Sensitivity Analysis

Present three scenarios:

| Scenario | Market Rent Assumption | Loss-to-Lease | Stabilized NOI | 5-Yr IRR |
|----------|----------------------|-----------------|-------------|----------|
| **Bear** | $1,150 (10th percentile) | $150 | $420K | 9% |
| **Base** | $1,200 (median) | $200 | $500K | 12% |
| **Bull** | $1,350 (75th percentile) | $350 | $600K | 16% |

**Conclusion**: "Even in the bear case, the property achieves acceptable returns. Base case supports 12% IRR."

#### 3. Acknowledge Deal Killers

If your analysis reveals a deal killer, state it clearly:

**Example – Rent Control Too Restrictive**:
> "San Francisco's 1.4% annual rent increase cap (vs. 6.8% statewide AB 1482 default) significantly limits value-add potential. The property's Loss-to-Lease of $200/unit/month cannot be captured through rent increases alone. Value-add relies entirely on lease turnover and vacant unit re-leasing. The lease expiration schedule shows 45% of units expiring within 3 years; however, 55% are locked in long-term leases (2024-2027+). Estimated stabilization timeline: 4-5 years vs. 2-3 years in less-regulated markets. IRR may be below target. Recommend: (a) negotiate lower purchase price to offset extended value-add timeline, or (b) pass on deal."

**Example – Demographic Mismatch**:
> "Local median household income ($38,000 annually) cannot support target market rent ($1,400/month, requiring $56,000+ household income). Property currently serves lower-income demographic; rent increase to market would price out current and replacement tenants. Recommend: (a) lower market rent assumption to $1,100 (30% of $44,000 household income), reducing Loss-to-Lease to $100/unit, or (b) reposition property to higher-income demographic (would require major renovation and different marketing)."

#### 4. Visual Aids

Include charts/graphs:
- **Rent by Unit Type** (bar chart): Current vs. Market vs. Adjusted
- **Lease Expiration Schedule** (line chart): Units turning per month over 3 years
- **Absorption Scenario** (line chart): Occupancy trajectory under different lease-up rates
- **Comp Adjustment Matrix** (table): Shows comp addresses, adjustments, final reconciled rent

## 7. INTERCONNECTIONS: HOW THIS CATEGORY SETS THE CEILING FOR INVESTMENT MATH & DEAL SOURCING

### 7.1 Revenue Ceiling: Setting the Pro Forma Rent

The Market Rent Study establishes the **maximum achievable rent** and the **timeline to achieve it**.

This becomes the input to:
- **Category 9 (Investment Math)**: Pro forma revenue assumes the market rent established here
- **Category 8 (Renovations & Capex)**: Renovation scope is sized to justify the market rent premium
- **Category 10 (Deal Sourcing)**: Deal sourcing targets properties with significant Loss-to-Lease AND favorable lease expiration profiles

**Example Flow**:
1. Market Analysis determines: Market rent = $1,200, Loss-to-Lease = $200, timeline = 3 years
2. Investment Math builds pro forma: Stabilized rent = $1,200; revenue grows 3-5% post-stabilization
3. Renovation plan specifies: $15,000/unit to include kitchen, flooring, appliances (justifies the $200 bump)
4. Deal Sourcing looks for: Properties with 40%+ units expiring within 3 years; rent currently $900-1,000 (proven demand, not aspirational)

### 7.2 Go/No-Go Decision in Deal Sourcing

The Market Rent Study often **kills the deal** early:

**Deal Sourcing encounters a prospect**:
- 25-unit, pre-1985 property
- Current rent: $900/month
- Seller asking price: $5M (implies $200K/unit)
- Initial business plan: "Renovate and move to market rent of $1,400"

**Market Rent Study reveals**:
- Local market rent (from comps): $1,100 (not $1,400)
- Loss-to-Lease: only $200/unit, not $500/unit
- Rent control: AB 1482 applies; $1,100 market rent must be phased in over lease turnovers
- Lease schedule: Only 30% of units expire in next 3 years; 70% locked in

**Conclusion**: Deal economics don't work at $5M ask price. Required offer price: ~$3.5M (lower cap rate assumption to reflect lower upside).

**Action**: Either renegotiate price or **pass on deal**.

## 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS & ACTION ITEMS

### Core Insights

1. **Loss-to-Lease is the forensic starting point.** Calculate it rigorously using independent CMA, not seller-provided "market rent." Reconcile against the T12 to ensure the numbers are real.

2. **Economic occupancy trumps physical occupancy.** A 95% physically occupied property with 8% delinquency is actually 87% economically occupied. Adjust pro formas accordingly.

3. **Rent control is a deal killer if it's not understood.** AB 1482's 5% + CPI cap (capped at 10%) is stricter than it sounds when applied to lease renewals. New York's Good Cause Eviction has NO vacant unit exemption, making value-add much harder. Always verify the specific local ordinance.

4. **Lease turnover is the value-add lever, not rent increases.** In rent-controlled jurisdictions, value-add is realized by capturing market rent when units turn over (vacant unit exemption). If 70% of units are locked in 5-year leases, stabilization takes 7+ years, not 2.

5. **Demographic affordability is a hard ceiling.** If local median income is $40,000/year, rent cannot sustainably go above $1,000/month (30% rule). Trying to force $1,300 rent will result in chronic vacancies and delinquencies.

6. **Absorption rate determines lease-up velocity.** In soft markets (<1% monthly absorption), a 50-unit renovation takes 18+ months to stabilize. In strong markets (2%+ monthly), it takes 6-9 months. Extend timeline accordingly and model cash flow impact.

7. **RUBS is a high-ROI operational lever.** If owner currently subsidizes utilities, implementing RUBS can add $500-1,500 NOI per unit annually. Verify legality in the specific jurisdiction (especially California rent-controlled cities).

8. **The Market Rent Study is not optional.** It is the lynchpin document that either validates the deal or reveals it's dead. Invest time in getting it right, with defensible sources and clear methodology.

### Pre-Offer Checklist: 5 Things to Verify Before Believing the Pro Forma Rent

**Before you submit an offer, complete this checklist**:

- [ ] **CMA is defensible**: You have identified 4-6 comparable properties within 1.5 miles, same vintage (±15 years), same unit mix. You have personally visited or called 3+ comps to confirm current asking/effective rents. You have adjusted for amenities using reasonable market values (+$25-100 per amenity). You have resolved any outlier comps (if one comp is 20%+ outside the range, you've investigated why).

- [ ] **Rent roll reconciles to T12**: The T12's "Gross Potential Rent" matches the rent roll's (average rent × units × 12 months). If variance >5%, you've investigated the discrepancy (late-year rent increase, occupancy change, data error). Bad debt and delinquency on the rent roll align with T12 expense figures.

- [ ] **Lease expiration schedule is mapped**: You have extracted all lease start and end dates from the rent roll. You have calculated the number of units expiring each month over the next 24-36 months. You have verified that the expiration schedule shows when you can actually *implement* the rent increase (no point projecting $1,200 rent if only 10% of units turn in the next 3 years).

- [ ] **Rent control is understood**: You have read the applicable state and local ordinance (AB 1482, local rent control ordinance, Good Cause Eviction law, etc.). You have identified the **maximum annual increase** and whether vacant unit re-leasing has an exemption. You have calculated whether the market rent can be achieved within the legal framework (e.g., if market rent is $1,200 and current rent is $1,000, and max increase is 5%, the rent will not reach $1,200 in a single year; it will take 4-5 years of stacked increases unless a unit turns vacant).

- [ ] **Demographic income supports the rent**: You have pulled Census Bureau American Community Survey data for the ZIP/market. You have calculated median household income and confirmed that the target rent ÷ 0.30 does not exceed local median income by >30%. If target rent is $1,400/month ($56,000 annual income required) and local median income is $40,000, you have downgraded the market rent assumption or explained the in-migration of higher-income residents.
