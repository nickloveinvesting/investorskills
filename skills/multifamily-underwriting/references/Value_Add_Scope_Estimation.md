# Deep Research Report: Value-Add Rehab & Scope Estimation for Vintage Multifamily Properties

## Executive Summary

The 2025-2026 renovation cost environment presents both opportunities and significant challenges for investors pursuing value-add strategies in pre-1990s multifamily buildings. With construction costs rising 6.6% year-over-year and multifamily development costs reaching $398 per square foot (a 75% increase from 2019), precision in scope estimation and cost forecasting has become the difference between profitable deals and capital destruction.

This report synthesizes current market data, contractor pricing, insurance requirements, and system remediation economics to provide investors with actionable frameworks for budgeting renovation projects in the 5-50 unit range. The critical distinction between "Market Ready" (light value-add) and "Value Add" (heavy repositioning) has compressed in today's market, where full-unit renovations averaging $15,000-$25,000 now compete with light cosmetic updates priced at $6,000-$10,000—yet the rent premium capture remains relatively flat across both tiers in many secondary markets.

**Bottom-Line Recommendation:** For pre-1990s vintage properties, allocate **15-20% contingency** on all hard costs to account for hidden defects, system failures, and material price volatility. The 10% standard is insufficient for buildings constructed between 1960 and 1990. Furthermore, insurance bindability requirements (aluminum wiring retrofit, polybutylene pipe replacement, FPE/Zinsco panel removal) are no longer optional; they are **mandatory capital expenditures** that must be front-loaded into the pro forma, not treated as "nice to haves."

## The Economics of "The Turn": Benchmarking Unit Costs

### Light Renovation Scope & Pricing

Light value-add renovations focus on rapid cosmetic recovery without system work. In the 2025-2026 market, light turns target units with intact systems and minimal structural issues. These are "refresh" plays—the property is functional but dated.

**Typical Light Renovation Scope:**
- Interior and exterior paint (standard color palette)
- Laminate or budget vinyl flooring (if replacement needed)
- New cabinet hardware and knobs (cabinet refacing avoided)
- Updated lighting fixtures and outlet covers
- Faucet and bathroom fixture refresh (no plumbing changes)
- Appliance refresh or energy-efficient replacements (like-for-like)
- Caulking, minor drywall repair, standard cleaning

**Cost Range: $6,000 - $10,000 per unit**

This baseline assumes approximately 750-850 square feet of rentable space. Material costs typically represent 40-50% of the hard cost budget, while skilled labor represents 30-40%. For light turns, material-to-labor ratio skews favorable, as much of the work involves straightforward installation rather than remediation.

**Per-Square-Foot Estimate:** $8-$13 per square foot for light turns in secondary markets (excludes system work).

### Heavy Renovation Scope & Pricing

Heavy value-add renovations target genuine repositioning. These projects address deferred maintenance, system upgrades, layout optimization, and significant amenity additions. They are capital-intensive but justify meaningful rent premiums in tighter markets.

**Typical Heavy Renovation Scope:**
- Complete kitchen remodel (cabinet replacement, countertop upgrade, appliance package)
- Bathroom modernization (fixtures, tile work, potential layout changes)
- Full flooring replacement (LVP or tile throughout unit)
- Interior paint and upgraded color schemes
- Electrical outlet/fixture modernization (assuming panel capacity sufficient)
- HVAC upgrades or efficiency improvements (if tenant-accessible components)
- Flooring transitions, baseboard replacement
- Smart home integration or tech upgrades (optional, market-dependent)
- Possible laundry hookup or appliance installation

**Cost Range: $20,000 - $35,000+ per unit**

The heavy scope escalates rapidly if original MEP system work is required (see "Vintage-Specific System Remediation" section below). Heavy turns justify 2-3 year planning horizons because the financial return depends on sustained rent capture.

**Per-Square-Foot Estimate:** $25-$45 per square foot for heavy turns in secondary markets (excluding major system replacement).

## Vintage Risk & System Remediation Costs

### Electrical System Remediation

Pre-1990s buildings present three primary electrical hazards that trigger insurance intervention: **aluminum wiring, undersized panels, and defective panel brands.**

#### Aluminum Wiring: AlumiConn vs. Full Rewire

**The Problem:** Homes built between 1965-1978 commonly used aluminum wiring to reduce costs. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission documented that aluminum-wired homes manufactured before 1972 are **55 times more likely** to experience fire hazard conditions than copper-wired homes. A typical property has 200+ electrical connections, exponentially increasing risk.

**Insurance Reality:** Insurance companies will **not** provide property or casualty coverage for buildings with untreated aluminum wiring. Some insurers now require affirmative documentation that the aluminum wiring has been "corrected" before they will bind a policy.

**Option 1: AlumiConn Retrofit (Partial Remediation)**
- **Process:** Licensed electrician installs AlumiConn or COPALUM connectors at all aluminum wire connection points. This prevents oxidation and loose connections without removing existing wiring.
- **Cost:** $1,500-$2,500 per property (full building retrofit, single-family equivalent). For multifamily, expect $150-$300 per unit for connection-level work, but many contractors bundle this into a single retrofit quote.
- **Timeline:** 1 day per property
- **Insurance Acceptance:** COPALUM and AlumiConn are CPSC-approved remediation methods. Most insurers will bind coverage after retrofit and inspection certification.
- **Pros:** Rapid, affordable, minimally disruptive, achieves insurance bindability
- **Cons:** Does not upgrade wiring capacity; leaves aluminum in place; if new circuits or major loads added, full rewire may eventually be necessary

**Option 2: Full Rewire (Complete Replacement)**
- **Process:** Licensed electrician removes all aluminum wiring and replaces with copper throughout the building.
- **Cost:** $10,000-$20,000+ per property depending on size and wall accessibility. For a typical 20-unit building, expect $50,000-$150,000 in total electrical costs.
- **Timeline:** 5-10 days depending on building complexity and wall access
- **Insurance Acceptance:** Full copper wiring eliminates aluminum wiring risk entirely; insurers have no concerns.
- **Pros:** Eliminates aluminum wiring risk entirely; allows capacity upgrades; future-proofs the property; no ongoing compliance concerns
- **Cons:** Expensive; disruptive; requires wall openings and patch/paint repair; extended timeline

**Renovation Economics:** For most 5-50 unit value-add deals, AlumiConn retrofit is the pragmatic choice. The cost differential ($1,500-$2,500 per property vs. $50,000+) justifies the retrofit path unless the property is also undergoing major electrical capacity upgrades (adding circuits, upgrading panels, adding electric cooking) that warrant full rewire.

**Action Item:** Budget AlumiConn retrofit as a **mandatory line item** if the building was constructed 1960-1980 and original wiring is visible. Do not gamble on insurance bindability without addressing this upfront.

#### Electrical Panel Replacement: FPE Stab-Lok and Zinsco Panels

**The Problem:** Federal Pacific Stab-Lok and Zinsco electrical panels were widely installed in pre-1980s buildings. Both have documented safety defects:
- **FPE Stab-Lok:** Breakers fail to trip in 40-60% of overload conditions, creating fire risk
- **Zinsco:** Breakers fuse to the bus bar, rendering them non-functional

Both panel types are no longer manufactured and are considered unsafe by the electrical industry.

**Insurance Reality:** Many insurers **will not renew** policies on properties with FPE or Zinsco panels. This is a hard stop for deal underwriting.

**Replacement Costs:**
- **Panel and breakers:** $800-$1,200 in materials
- **Labor:** $1,500-$2,500 (includes removal, installation, circuit breaker reconnection, permitting, inspection)
- **Total per panel:** $3,500-$4,000 average (Denver metro example: $6,000-$10,000 for full service upgrade)
- **For multifamily:** Budget $3,500-$4,000 per main panel; most 5-50 unit buildings have 1-2 main panels

**Timeline:** 4-8 hours of power downtime; project completion 1-2 days including permits and inspection

**Contingency Planning:** If the building has a known FPE or Zinsco panel, add the replacement cost to **acquisition pro forma**, not to renovation budget. Many deals fall apart if this cost is discovered post-acquisition. Inspect the electrical panel during due diligence.

### Plumbing System Remediation

Pre-1990s buildings commonly used two problematic piping materials: **polybutylene (plastic) and galvanized steel.** Both require replacement in renovation deals.

#### Polybutylene (PB) Piping Replacement

**The Problem:** Polybutylene piping was widely used 1978-1995 before being phased out due to documented failures. The pipes degrade from oxidation, becoming brittle and prone to failure. Mass litigation occurred in the 1990s; an $850 million class action settlement was paid by manufacturers.

**Insurance Reality:** Many insurance companies **refuse coverage** or **cancel policies** for properties with polybutylene piping. Some carriers will only provide coverage if the piping is completely replaced.

**Replacement Option: PEX (Cross-Linked Polyethylene)**

PEX is the modern, cost-effective standard. It's flexible, won't corrode, resists chlorine buildup, and won't freeze.

**Costs per Linear Foot:**
- **PEX material:** $0.50-$2.00 per linear foot
- **Installation labor:** Varies by complexity, but typically $1.50-$3.00 per linear foot
- **Total installed:** $2.00-$5.00 per linear foot (low end) to $4.00-$5.00 per linear foot (high end, complex routing)

**Total Project Costs by Home Size:**
- **1,000 sq ft (8-10 fixtures):** $4,000-$5,500
- **1,500-2,000 sq ft (10-12 fixtures):** $5,500-$8,000
- **2,000-2,500 sq ft (12-15 fixtures):** $7,000-$10,000

**For Multifamily (per unit estimate):** Assume 800-900 sq ft units with 8-10 fixtures average $4,500-$6,500 per unit for complete PEX repipe.

**Timeline:** 2-3 days per unit, assuming walls are accessible

**Contingency:** Expect 10-20% cost overrun if galvanized steel fittings are heavily corroded, requiring extended labor for removal and remediation. Budget accordingly.

#### Galvanized Steel Piping Upgrade

**The Problem:** Galvanized steel piping, common in pre-1990s buildings, accumulates mineral deposits and corrosion over decades, reducing water flow and increasing replacement urgency.

**Replacement to PEX:**
- **Typical cost premium:** 10-20% higher than standard PEX repipe due to difficult removal of corroded fittings and potential water damage repair
- **Cost range:** $6,000-$10,000 for typical apartment unit (larger than standard repipe due to remediation labor)
- **For 20-unit building:** $120,000-$200,000 in plumbing system replacement

**Action Item:** Budget galvanized-to-PEX replacement as **critical capital expense** for any pre-1980s property. Water quality complaints and emergency repairs become increasingly costly post-acquisition.

### Environmental Hazards: Lead and Asbestos

#### Lead Paint Remediation

**The Problem:** Homes built before 1978 commonly contain lead-based paint. Lead exposure poses health risks, particularly to young children. Property owners are legally liable for lead disclosure and may face compliance issues.

**Remediation Options:**

**Option 1: Full Removal**
- **Cost:** $10-$17 per square foot
- **Total for average unit (850 sq ft):** $8,500-$14,450
- **Labor-intensive:** Removal is specialized work requiring certified lead abatement contractors
- **Pros:** Eliminates lead hazard entirely
- **Cons:** Most expensive; creates disposal costs

**Option 2: Encapsulation**
- **Cost:** $6-$10 per square foot (15-25% cheaper than removal)
- **Total for average unit:** $5,100-$8,500
- **Process:** Professional sealant is applied over lead paint, creating a barrier
- **Pros:** Lower cost; faster; effective if well-maintained
- **Cons:** Requires long-term maintenance; sealant can fail and expose lead

**Option 3: Enclosure (Minimal)**
- **Cost:** $8-$16 per square foot
- **Process:** Physical barriers (drywall, paneling) placed over lead paint
- **Pros:** Cost-effective for large areas
- **Cons:** Covers but doesn't eliminate hazard

**Typical Renovation Approach:** For multifamily value-add, **encapsulation** is often the pragmatic choice when lead paint is identified during renovation. The cost ($6-$10/sq ft) is manageable and preserves the ability to paint over trim and surfaces being updated anyway. Full removal is reserved for high-impact areas (kitchens, bathrooms, heavily trafficked areas).

**Lead Paint Inspection Costs:** $240-$439 average (budget $300-$400 for initial testing)

#### Asbestos Abatement

**The Problem:** Asbestos was commonly used in insulation, floor tiles, roofing, and HVAC ductwork in pre-1980s buildings. Disturbance during renovation can release dangerous fibers.

**Remediation Costs:**
- **Interior areas:** $5-$20 per square foot (depending on material and removal vs. encapsulation)
- **Exterior areas:** $50-$150 per square foot
- **HVAC ductwork:** $35-$55 per square foot (difficult access)
- **Whole-home abatement:** $5,700+ average

**Budget Strategy:** If asbestos is identified in non-critical areas (exterior siding, underground pipes), **encapsulation** is often chosen over removal. Encapsulation costs 15-25% less and is acceptable if the material won't be disturbed.

**Critical Timeline:** Asbestos and lead testing/remediation should be completed **before renovation planning**, as the results drive budget assumptions and trade sequencing.

## Budget Construction: Hard Costs vs. Soft Costs

### Definitions and Cost Architecture

Understanding the distinction between hard and soft costs is essential for building reliable pro formas.

**Hard Costs** are tangible construction expenses:
- Materials (lumber, drywall, flooring, fixtures, appliances)
- Labor (wages for general labor and skilled trades)
- Subcontractor services (plumbing, electrical, HVAC)
- Equipment rental and temporary facilities
- Permits and inspections (directly tied to construction)
- Dumpsters and disposal

**Soft Costs** are indirect, non-construction expenses:
- Architectural and engineering fees
- Loan interest and financing costs
- Project management and overhead
- Insurance and bonding (project-level)
- Utility connections and temporary service
- Legal fees and title work
- Tenant relocation or holding costs
- Marketing and leasing commissions (post-renovation)
- General contingency reserves

### Hard Costs: Detailed Breakdown

For a typical 20-unit multifamily value-add project with average 850 sq ft units, hard cost categories should be broken down as follows:

**Unit Interior Renovations** (Light to Mid-Range)
- Paint (interior/exterior): 3-5% of unit renovation budget
- Flooring: 20-25% (LVP typical)
- Kitchen (cabinets, counters, appliances): 25-35%
- Bathroom fixtures and tile: 15-20%
- Lighting, electrical outlets: 5-7%
- Doors, hardware, trim: 5-8%
- Appliances and fixtures: 10-15%
- Drywall repair, caulking, misc: 3-5%

**Total per unit (light):** $6,000-$10,000
**Total per unit (heavy):** $20,000-$35,000+

**Building Systems (If Required)**
- Electrical panel replacement: $3,500-$4,000 per panel
- Aluminum wiring retrofit: $1,500-$2,500 per building
- Plumbing repipe (full): $4,500-$7,000 per unit
- HVAC upgrades: $2,000-$4,000 per unit (if major work)
- Roof repair/replacement: $5-$15 per square foot
- Exterior painting: $2-$4 per square foot

**Environmental Remediation**
- Lead abatement (encapsulation): $6-$10 per square foot of affected area
- Asbestos encapsulation: $2-$6 per square foot
- Mold remediation (if discovered): $15-$30 per square foot

### Soft Costs: Categories and Benchmarks

**Architectural and Design Fees**
- For value-add projects, typically 2-5% of total hard costs
- For a $300,000 renovation (20 units × $15,000), budget $6,000-$15,000
- May be lower if using standard design templates vs. custom layouts

**Permitting and Inspections**
- City permits vary widely by jurisdiction but typically 1-3% of hard costs
- Budget $3,000-$9,000 for 20-unit project
- Lead/asbestos certifications: $1,000-$3,000 additional

**Loan Interest (Holding Costs)**
- If financing renovation at 7-8% interest on $300,000 hard costs
- For 12-month construction period: $21,000-$24,000 in interest
- **This is substantial** and often overlooked in pro formas

**Project Management**
- For 5-50 unit deals, owner-operator management costs 2-5% of hard costs
- Alternatively, hire project manager at $3,000-$5,000 per month for 3-6 month project

**Insurance and Bonding**
- General liability during renovation: $1,500-$3,000 for project period
- Performance bonds (if contractor-required): 1-3% of hard costs

**Utilities and Temporary Services**
- Dumpster rental (entire project): $3,000-$5,000
- Temporary power/water: $2,000-$4,000
- Temporary fencing or security: $1,000-$3,000

**Holdover/Carrying Costs**
- Property taxes during renovation: Pro-rata (if not exempted)
- Insurance (standard coverage, not project): Ongoing per month
- Utilities (common areas): $500-$1,000 per month × number of months

**Contingency (Hard Costs)**
- Standard new construction: 5-10%
- Value-add renovation: 10-15%
- **Vintage property renovation: 15-20% (CRITICAL for pre-1990s buildings)**

### Soft Cost Load as Percentage of Total Project

**Typical Breakdown for 5-50 Unit Multifamily Value-Add:**
- Hard costs: 65-75% of total project costs
- Soft costs: 25-35% of total project costs

**Example: 20-Unit Building, $15,000 per-unit renovation ($300,000 hard costs)**

| Cost Category | Amount | % of Total |
|---------------|--------|-----------|
| Hard Costs (base) | $300,000 | 65% |
| Hard Costs Contingency (15%) | $45,000 | 10% |
| Architectural/Design (3%) | $9,000 | 2% |
| Permitting (2%) | $6,000 | 1.3% |
| Loan Interest (12-month @ 7%) | $21,000 | 4.5% |
| Project Management (3%) | $9,000 | 2% |
| Insurance/Bonding | $2,500 | 0.5% |
| Utilities/Dumpster | $5,000 | 1% |
| Holdover/Carrying (6 months) | $8,000 | 1.7% |
| **Total Project Cost** | **$405,500** | **100%** |

**Cost per unit:** $20,275 total ($15,000 hard + $5,275 soft)

This example illustrates why projects frequently exceed budget by 15-25%: soft costs and contingency must be calculated on the full project scope, not just hard costs.

### Contingency Strategy for Vintage Assets

**The 15-20% Rule for Pre-1990s Buildings:**

Standard 10% contingency is insufficient for pre-1990s properties. These buildings frequently harbor hidden defects that don't surface until walls are opened:

**Common "Behind the Walls" Discoveries:**
- Corroded/burst pipes not visible from unit level (requires partial replumbing: $1,000-$3,000 per discovery)
- Aluminum wiring in unexpected locations (requires additional AlumiConn work: $200-$500 per location)
- Asbestos in floor tiles, ceiling tiles, or pipe wrap (requires abatement: $5,000-$15,000)
- Mold in walls or HVAC systems (requires remediation: $2,000-$10,000+)
- Structural damage, rotted wood, or settling (requires structural repair: highly variable)
- Inadequate insulation, leading to code compliance issues (adds $2,000-$5,000 per unit)

**Contingency Allocation Strategy:**
- **0-5%:** Cosmetic/paint-focused projects, low-risk
- **10-15%:** Standard renovation with visible scope, minor systems work anticipated
- **15-20%:** Vintage property renovation with aging systems requiring selective remediation
- **20%+:** Full system overhaul, significant structural concerns, or unknown subsurface conditions

**Action Item:** When underwriting a vintage property deal, **budget 15-20% contingency as a line item in the hard costs**, not as a "if needed" reserve. Treat it as committed capital.

## Advanced Value-Add: ADUs and Amenity ROI

### Feasibility of ADU Retrofits

Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU) conversion is an increasingly viable path to increase unit density in 5-50 unit buildings, particularly in high-rent markets where the rent premium from an additional unit justifies conversion costs.

**Most Common ADU Conversion Types for Multifamily:**

**1. Garage Conversion to ADU**
- **Prerequisites:** Detached garage or semi-detached carport structure with roof and walls in place
- **Scope:** Convert 240-400 sq ft garage space into independent studio/1-bedroom ADU
- **Cost:** $15,000-$50,000 depending on finishes and utility complexity
- **Cost per sq ft:** $100-$250 per square foot
- **Timeline:** 2-4 months

**Garage ADU Cost Breakdown (300 sq ft example):**
- Foundation/floor prep: $2,000-$3,000
- Framing, insulation, drywall: $3,000-$5,000
- Roofing/weatherproofing: $2,000-$3,000
- Electrical (new panel/circuits): $2,500-$4,000
- Plumbing (kitchen, bathroom): $3,500-$6,000
- Finishes (flooring, paint, kitchen cabinet): $4,000-$6,000
- Permits, inspections: $1,500-$2,500
- Contingency (15%): $3,000-$4,500
- **Total:** $21,500-$34,000

**ROI Timeline:** If an ADU rents for $1,200-$1,500/month, annual rent = $14,400-$18,000. After maintenance/taxes (~$2,400/year), net = $12,000-$15,600 annually. On $25,000 investment, **payback = 1.6-2.1 years**, then pure cash flow thereafter.

**2. Basement ADU Conversion**
- **Prerequisites:** Existing basement or semi-basement with 7+ ft clearance, separate entrance feasible
- **Scope:** Convert basement into independent living unit with separate utilities
- **Cost:** $20,000-$40,000 (higher due to bathroom plumbing, waterproofing, egress)
- **Cost per sq ft:** $150-$300 per square foot
- **Regulatory Risk:** Higher permitting risk; many jurisdictions restrict basement living units

**3. Existing Accessory Structure (Laundry Room, Storage)**
- **Prerequisites:** Separate building or large interior space currently non-income-producing
- **Scope:** Convert to efficient studio ADU (400-500 sq ft)
- **Cost:** $25,000-$50,000
- **Best Case:** Conversion of existing laundry facility to ADU, relocating laundry to main building (adds revenue while freeing space)

### In-Unit Laundry: The Rent Premium Driver

In-unit laundry is one of the highest-ROI amenities in multifamily. The data is clear:

**Market Rent Premiums:**
- **In-unit washer/dryer:** 6% rent premium compared to properties without in-unit laundry
- **Dollar premium:** $50-$150 per month depending on market tier and existing rent

**Cost Structure:**
- **Washer/dryer appliances:** $800-$2,000 (mid-range stack units)
- **Installation:** $100-$300 per unit (hookup only, assuming utilities in place)
- **Electrical upgrade (if needed):** $200-$500 per unit (new outlet or subpanel)
- **Plumbing upgrade (if needed):** $300-$800 per unit (vent line, water lines)
- **Total per unit:** $1,200-$3,600

**ROI Calculation:**
- Monthly rent premium: $50-$150 (use $100 as midpoint)
- Annual additional rent: $1,200
- Appliance cost: $1,500 average
- **Payback period: 1.25 years** (just from appliance cost, before installation premium captures)
- Post-payback cash flow: $1,200/year in perpetuity (minus utility increases, maintenance)

**Decision Framework:**
- **Include in renovation if:** Unit can accommodate hookups without major rework and market supports premium
- **Skip if:** Plumbing/electrical modifications exceed $1,000 per unit OR market rent premiums are negligible (<$30/month)
- **Hybrid approach:** Install in first floor or easily accessible units; phase installation in subsequent turns

**Financing Note:** Some investors use utility billback systems, where the cost of in-unit laundry is offset by capturing the difference in water/sewer costs. This requires separate metering.

### Green/Efficiency Retrofits: Water & Energy Conservation

Water conservation measures offer both **soft-cost appeal** (ESG/sustainability positioning) and **hard-cost ROI** (utility bill reduction).

**Most Cost-Effective Measures:**

**Water Conservation:**
- Low-flow shower heads: $20-$50 per head, $1-$3 installation
- Toilet rebuild (low-flush conversion): $100-$200 per toilet, 30-minute labor
- Faucet aerators: $10-$25 per unit, minimal labor
- **Total per unit:** $300-$600
- **Annual water savings:** $200-$400 per unit (depending on regional water costs)
- **Payback period:** 1-2 years

**Utility Monitoring & Billback Systems:**
- Sub-metering for water, electric (allows tenant billing)
- Installation cost: $500-$1,500 per unit
- Capture utility cost savings (typically 15-25% reduction in owner-paid utilities)
- ROI: 2-3 years if combined with conservation measures

**HVAC Efficiency (Tenant-Level):**
- Programmable thermostat: $100-$300 per unit
- Duct sealing (if accessible): $300-$800 per unit
- **Annual savings:** $200-$400 per unit
- **Payback:** 1-2 years

**Action Item:** For value-add deals, prioritize **water conservation** as a low-cost, high-impact amenity. Bundle low-flow fixtures into every unit renovation at modest cost; savings accrue immediately and are attractive to sustainability-minded tenants.

## Execution & Logistics

### General Contractor vs. Owner-Operator Subcontractor Management

The choice between hiring a GC (General Contractor) and managing subcontractors directly has profound implications for cost, control, and timeline.

**General Contractor Model:**
- **Structure:** Single point of accountability; GC hires, manages, and pays all subcontractors
- **Cost:** GC markup typically 10-15% of hard costs (can be 20%+ for small projects or competitive bids)
- **Example:** $300,000 hard costs × 12.5% GC fee = $37,500 in GC overhead
- **Pros:** Single point of contact; GC assumes liability for schedule and quality; clear change order process
- **Cons:** Higher cost; less control over subcontractor selection; GC incentives may not align with owner cost minimization

**Owner-Operator (Subcontractor Management) Model:**
- **Structure:** Owner/property manager directly hires electrician, plumber, framing, etc.; owns schedule and coordination
- **Cost:** No GC markup; owner saves 10-15% vs. GC model, but absorbs project management labor
- **Example:** $300,000 hard costs - $37,500 GC fee = $262,500 direct spend; owner manages instead
- **Pros:** Cost savings; direct control over subcontractor selection and quality; alignment with owner priorities
- **Cons:** Requires active management; owner liable for schedule slippage; more complex coordination; personal liability for safety/compliance

**Decision Framework:**

| Scenario | Recommended Approach | Rationale |
|----------|---------------------|-----------|
| First-time renovator, 1-2 properties | General Contractor | Turnkey simplicity worth 10-15% premium |
| Experienced operator, 3+ properties/year | Owner-Operator + PM | Cost savings exceed management complexity |
| 5-unit or fewer | Owner-Operator | Small enough to manage directly |
| 20+ units, complex timeline | General Contractor | Coordination complexity justifies GC overhead |
| Mixed portfolio, some simple, some complex | Hybrid (GC for complex, owner-op for routine) | Optimize cost on routine; control on complex |

**Cost-Plus vs. Fixed Price Contracting:**

If using a GC or primary contractor, contract structure matters significantly.

**Fixed Price / Lump Sum:**
- Contractor quotes all-in price for defined scope
- Owner risk: Minimal (contractor bears overrun risk)
- Contractor risk: High (incentive to cut corners or underestimate)
- **Cost:** Often 15-20% higher due to contractor risk premium
- **Best for:** Well-defined, straightforward scopes with minimal unknowns

**Cost-Plus (Time & Materials):**
- Contractor bills actual costs + management fee (typically 8-15%)
- Owner risk: Higher (changes/overages flow through)
- Contractor risk: Lower (paid for actual work)
- **Cost:** 5-10% lower IF owner maintains tight cost control
- **Best for:** Vintage properties with uncertain behind-walls conditions; value-add with potential scope changes

**Owner-Operator Tip:** If managing subcontractors directly, use **cost-plus with controls**: each subcontractor quotes hourly rate + materials. Lock in rate agreements with top 3-4 trade partners and bid major projects among them to maintain competitive pressure.

### Phasing Renovations in Occupied Buildings

For properties with existing tenants, renovation phasing is critical to maintain cash flow and minimize disruption.

**Phasing Strategy 1: Unit-by-Unit Turnover (Recommended for Occupied Buildings)**
- Complete one unit renovation at a time
- Conduct each renovation during normal tenant turnover (30-60 days between leases)
- Pros: Maintains occupancy; rent increases begin unit-by-unit; lower coordination complexity
- Cons: Slower overall timeline (5-50 units = 6-18 months); subcontractor scheduling spread
- **Timeline Impact:** 20 units × 10 days per unit = 200 construction days = 6-9 months with normal crew continuity

**Phasing Strategy 2: Blitz Renovation (Full Building, Rapid)**
- Accelerate through mass unit renovations over 6-12 weeks
- Execute all units simultaneously with multiple crews
- Pros: Faster overall timeline; economies of scale on material purchasing; market repositioning quicker
- Cons: Requires brief full vacancy; higher coordination burden; larger upfront capital draw
- **Best for:** Buildings with low occupancy or where extended vacancy is planned anyway

**Phasing Strategy 3: Floor-by-Floor (Mixed Occupied/Vacant)**
- Coordinate by floor: complete one floor at a time
- Each floor is fully vacated for 2-4 weeks; tenant temporarily relocated
- Pros: Moderate coordination; faster than unit-by-unit; maintains partial occupancy
- Cons: Requires relocation logistics; potential tenant friction

**Occupied-Building Cash Flow Preservation:**
- Begin renovations on **first units to turn naturally** (upcoming lease expirations)
- Prioritize **highest-rent units** (early rent premiums capture)
- **Maintain occupancy throughout:** Never exceed 20% vacancy unless strategic
- **Monthly carry cost:** Every month of additional timeline costs $5,000-$10,000+ in mortgage, taxes, insurance per 20-unit building

**Action Item:** For occupied value-add deals, model renovation phasing explicitly in pro forma. Timeline sensitivity is often the difference between 15% and 25% IRR.

## Interconnections: How Scoping, Due Diligence, and Market Analysis Converge

Accurate renovation scoping does not exist in isolation. It is directly dependent on thorough vintage-specific due diligence and must be validated against market rent achievement.

**Due Diligence Findings → Scope Definition:**
- If electrical inspection reveals aluminum wiring requiring AlumiConn retrofit, scoping adds $1,500-$2,500 per building (non-negotiable for insurance)
- If plumbing inspection identifies polybutylene, scoping adds $4,500-$7,000 per unit for complete repipe
- If roof is aging and has 5-7 years remaining life, renovation pro forma must assume roof replacement timing (either now or in Year 3 capital plan)

**Scope Ambition → Market Rent Validation:**
- Proposing $25,000 per-unit renovation requires market evidence that rents will sustain the premium
- If comparables show $1,900 as market rent and you're achieving $2,000-$2,100 (5-10% premium), the renovation is justified
- If market is flat at $1,900 and your renovation commands only $1,950 (2.6% premium), the ROI may not justify the cost—consider lightening scope

**Loss-to-Lease Capture Strategy:**
- If property is currently 15% below-market due to age and condition, the renovation targets loss-to-lease recovery
- First $100-150/month of rent increase is recovery of market gap; only rents beyond that are "value-add premium"
- If market rent is $2,000, but current unit is leasing at $1,800, the first $200/month recovery is expected from any renovated unit repositioning

## Common Budget Busters: Pre-1960-1990 Buildings

Based on practitioner experience, these discoveries frequently derail pre-1990s renovation budgets:

1. **Polybutylene Pipes (1978-1995 Construction):** Failed pipes require emergency repair or full system replacement. Budget pressure occurs when partial failures are discovered mid-renovation, forcing decision to repipe or patch-repair.

2. **HVAC System Failure:** Aging systems become unreliable during renovation. Temporary cooling/heating becomes necessary. Budget impact: $2,000-$5,000 for temporary HVAC + potential emergency repair to existing system.

3. **Roof Leaks Discovered During Remodel:** Opening walls may reveal water damage or previous leaks. Roof remediation becomes priority. Budget impact: $3,000-$15,000 depending on extent.

4. **Asbestos in Unexpected Locations:** Ceiling tiles, floor tiles, pipe wrap, or HVAC ductwork contain asbestos. Discovery mid-project requires work stoppage and abatement. Budget impact: $5,000-$20,000.

5. **Load-Bearing Wall Misidentification:** Wall deemed non-load-bearing turns out to be structural. Requires engineer redesign and alternative bracing. Budget impact: $3,000-$10,000.

6. **Insufficient Electrical Capacity:** During renovation, plans to add circuits or appliances reveal that panel is maxed. Panel upgrade required (costly). Budget impact: $3,500-$6,000.

7. **Mold in HVAC or Walls:** Hidden mold discovered during renovation requires remediation before proceeding. Budget impact: $2,000-$10,000.

8. **Code Compliance Failures:** Egress, fire separation, or other code issues discovered during renovation require remediation. Budget impact: $2,000-$15,000 depending on violation.

**Mitigation Strategy:** Budget the 15-20% contingency to absorb these discoveries. Prioritize **pre-renovation inspection and testing** (electrical, plumbing, asbestos, lead, structural) to surface issues before hard costs are committed.

## Key Takeaways & Action Items

### Ten Essential Budgeting Rules for Pre-1990s Multifamily Value-Add

1. **Allocate 15-20% Contingency as Committed Capital** — Not a "hope it's not needed" reserve. Treat contingency as mandatory budget line item for vintage properties.

2. **Electrical Panel Replacement is Insurance Bindability Requirement** — FPE or Zinsco panels must be replaced ($3,500-$4,000 per panel). Do not defer; it's a deal-killer if insurance won't bind.

3. **Aluminum Wiring Retrofit is Non-Negotiable** — AlumiConn retrofit ($1,500-$2,500) is minimum for insurance coverage. Budget it as hard cost, not contingency.

4. **Polybutylene Piping Requires Full Replacement** — Do not attempt spot repairs. Budget $4,500-$7,000 per unit for complete PEX repipe.

5. **Light Turns ($6,000-$10,000) Have Compressed ROI** — Paint, flooring, fixtures yield only 2-5% rent premiums in flat markets. Ensure rent comps justify spend before committing to light scope.

6. **Cabinet Refacing (Not Replacement) Drives Value-Add ROI** — Spend $4,000-$6,000 on refacing instead of $10,000+ on replacement. Visual impact is 90% of the benefit at 50% of the cost.

7. **Soft Costs Typically 25-35% of Total Project** — If hard costs are $300,000, budget $100,000-$140,000 in soft costs (architectural, permits, interest, project management, contingency). Many investors underestimate this load.

8. **In-Unit Laundry Payback is 12-18 Months** — At $1,200-$1,500 per unit cost and $50-$150 monthly rent premium, in-unit laundry is one of the few amenities with clear mathematical ROI. Prioritize if utilities are in place.

9. **Phasing Matters More Than Speed** — Unit-by-unit renovation preserves cash flow and maintains occupancy. Faster is not always better if it requires extended vacancy.

10. **Owner-Operator Subcontractor Management Saves 10-15%** — If capacity exists to manage subcontractors directly, the cost savings exceed the management burden for experienced operators.

### Initial Walkthrough Scoping Checklist

Use this checklist during property tours to identify system-level risks and hard cost drivers:

**Electrical:**
- [ ] Identify main panel brand. Is it FPE, Zinsco, or other non-standard? → If yes, budget $3,500-$4,000 replacement
- [ ] Check visible wiring in attic/basement. Is it aluminum? → If yes, budget $1,500-$2,500 AlumiConn retrofit
- [ ] Note panel amperage. Is it 60 or 100 amps? → If 60 amps, limit renovation scope (cannot support modern loads)

**Plumbing:**
- [ ] Identify main water lines. Are they copper, PVC, galvanized steel, or plastic/polybutylene? → If galvanized or PB, budget $4,500-$7,000 per unit repipe to PEX
- [ ] Check for active leaks, water damage, or rust stains indicating corrosion
- [ ] Note whether drain lines are accessible (critical for rough-in cost)

**Mechanical (HVAC/Heating):**
- [ ] Check age and condition of furnace/boiler. Is it original 1960s? → If yes, plan replacement within 3 years
- [ ] Note whether building has central HVAC or individual units per apartment → Affects renovation feasibility and cost

**Environmental:**
- [ ] Check for lead paint (visual chipping or professional testing) → If present, budget $6-$10 per sq ft encapsulation per affected unit
- [ ] Check for asbestos (pipe wrap, floor tiles, ceiling tiles) → If suspected, budget professional abatement testing
- [ ] Check for mold, water stains, or pest damage → Indicates maintenance issues and potential remediation costs

**Structural & Building Envelope:**
- [ ] Check roof condition from exterior. Is it sagging, missing shingles, or clearly aging? → If poor, plan roof replacement ($5-$15 per sq ft)
- [ ] Check for foundation cracks, bowing walls, or visible settling → Major cost implications
- [ ] Check windows. Are they original single-pane? → Budget for window replacement or secondary glazing

**Walkthrough Output:**
- Estimate hard costs by category (electrical, plumbing, renovation, systems, environmental)
- Flag non-negotiable costs (panel replacement, wiring retrofit, plumbing repipe, hazard abatement)
- Identify optional value-add upgrades (cabinet refacing, in-unit laundry, flooring, fixtures)
- Calculate total project cost (hard + 15-20% contingency + soft costs)
- Validate against market rent assumptions and underwriting hurdle rate

## Conclusion

Precision in renovation scoping is the foundation of profitable value-add investing. In the 2025-2026 market, where construction inflation remains elevated (6.6% YoY) and financing costs remain high (7-8% for construction debt), the difference between a 12% IRR deal and a 20% IRR deal often comes down to **discipline in scope definition and realistic contingency planning.**

For pre-1990s buildings, the core principle is simple: **Identify and budget for system remediation first; optimize cosmetic value-add second.** Insurance bindability requirements (aluminum wiring retrofit, panel replacement, polybutylene repipe) are non-negotiable capital commitments that must be front-loaded. Once systems are remediated, the value-add ROI story (cabinet refacing, flooring, in-unit laundry) can drive rent premiums and justify project returns.

The investors who succeed in this market are those who treat contingency as committed capital, understand the hard cost vs. soft cost breakdown, and validate renovation scopes against realistic market rent achievement. Underestimating by 20% in this environment destroys returns. Overestimating scope and spending on amenities that don't yield rent premiums equally destroys returns. The path to consistent 15%+ IRR is methodical, data-driven scoping—not guesswork.
